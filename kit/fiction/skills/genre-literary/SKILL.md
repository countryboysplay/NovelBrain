# Literary fiction

Layers on top of the audience profile. Where this skill and the audience profile disagree on length or content limits, the audience profile wins. Literary fiction is a market category more than a formula, so these rules describe reader expectations, not a template.

## Reader promise
- LIT-PROM-01: Promise an inward study of the human condition. Events matter because of how they affect the characters, so tie each major event to a visible change in a character's inner state. [S1]
- LIT-PROM-02: The tone is described as introspective, and the category as a study of the human condition and of difficult social or political issues, so record the governing theme as a question in the Story notes section of `bible/world/brief.md` (house setting). [S1]
- LIT-PROM-03: The promise includes the quality of the prose. Literary fiction is described as driven by craft and the quality of the language, and it moves more slowly because the language gets so much attention. [S2]
- LIT-PROM-04: Literary fiction aims to resemble real life and to show real-life reactions to complex events through developed characters, so keep reactions psychologically plausible. [S3]

## Structure
- LIT-STR-01: A story arc may or may not be present, so decide on purpose whether the book has a conventional arc and record that choice in the Story notes section of `bible/world/brief.md` (house setting). [S1]
- LIT-STR-02: A quiet book still needs a shape. One commenter relays a workshop idea of plot as shape, and an inner state such as unadmitted loneliness can count as the thing that happens, so name the shape of the character's change in one sentence in the Story notes section of `bible/world/brief.md` (house setting). [S6]
- LIT-STR-03: Quiet stories draw their drama from small shifts and tend to reflect instead of resolve, so make each chapter turn on a small, nameable shift. [S6]
- LIT-STR-04: Open endings are common in literary fiction and a satisfying ending is no guarantee, so choose the ending mode (resolved, open or ambiguous) and record it in the Ending section of `bible/world/brief.md` before drafting the last act (house setting). [S2][S1]
- LIT-STR-05: The opening should establish who the reader is following, what is at stake and what conflict will drive the story. It need not open with the conflict itself. [S5]

## Conventions
- LIT-CONV-01: Interiority shows why characters act. It stays private and often contrasts with what the character says or does aloud. [S4]
- LIT-CONV-02: Pick the interiority technique on purpose: stream of consciousness, internal monologue, free indirect discourse or direct narration. Free indirect discourse folds the character's thoughts into third-person narration. [S4]
- LIT-CONV-03: The line between hero and villain is often blurry, so do not force characters into clean good and bad roles. [S1]
- LIT-CONV-04: Without a tidy plot to explain motives, metaphor, symbolism and imagery carry more of the storytelling, so let recurring images do thematic work. [S1]
- LIT-CONV-05: Voice should be distinct to the writer or narrator and should show on the first page. One agent says it is either on the page or it is not. [S5]
- LIT-CONV-06: Themes are complex and aim at life lessons and deeper meanings, so avoid a single moral that closes the question. [S3]

## Pitfalls
- LIT-PIT-01: Too little interiority leaves a manuscript of dialogue and action where readers may stop caring. Too much slows the pace and reads as boring. Balance the two. [S4]
- LIT-PIT-02: Too much background, history or world-building early, and characters the reader does not need yet, undermine the opening. [S5]
- LIT-PIT-03: Without genre conventions to lean on, literary writers often struggle to tell a complete, compelling story, so check that the book still resolves its central question or knowingly leaves it open. [S3]
- LIT-PIT-04: An agent rejected an early draft of a quiet book as one where nothing happens. The author later called that fair, since agents need to see a commercial future as well as feel passion. [S6]
- LIT-PIT-05: For books on the upmarket side of the range, heavy capital-L prose works against the category, and unlikable protagonists risk readers not finishing. The source is about upmarket fiction, so treat this as guidance for literary-leaning commercial books. [S2]

## Reader psychology
Builds on the reader-engagement skill.
- LIT-ENG-01: Every scene gives at least one sentence of the POV character's private thought, feeling or perception that is not already stated in their dialogue or action (house setting). [S4]
  Check: point to one sentence in the scene that reveals an inner reaction the character does not say aloud.
- LIT-ENG-02: No unbroken run of pure reflection longer than 250 words without an outward action, line of dialogue or concrete sensory detail (house setting). [S4]
  Check: scan each interior passage and count its words between outward beats; the check fails if any run exceeds 250 words.
- LIT-ENG-03: The opening pages (first 500 words, house setting) identify whose perspective we hold and what that person wants or risks. [S5]
  Check: within the first 500 words there is a sentence naming a want, fear or risk for the POV character.
- LIT-ENG-04: Each chapter makes the protagonist choose something, even a small thing, rather than only think. Inner conflicts and choices can generate suspense. [S4]
  Check: name one choice the protagonist makes on the page in the chapter.
- LIT-ENG-05: Each chapter ends with an open question about the character, such as what they want, fear or will do, since quiet stories reflect instead of resolve (house setting). [S6]
  Check: the last 150 words leave a specific unresolved feeling, choice or relationship question that you can write in one sentence.

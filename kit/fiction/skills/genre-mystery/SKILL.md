# Mystery

Layers on top of the audience profile. Where this skill and the audience profile disagree on length or content limits, the audience profile wins. The core contract is a puzzle the reader has a fair chance to solve.

## Reader promise
- MYS-PROM-01: Readers come for a brain-teasing puzzle. They want to be fooled but also want a real chance to crack the case, so suspense plus a final reveal is not enough. [S3]
- MYS-PROM-02: A mystery is a contest of wits between reader and author, and the reader must have access to every clue. [S2]
- MYS-PROM-03: The best ending gives a solution that surprises readers but was visible all along. Aim for readers to think they should have seen it coming. [S5][S1]
- MYS-PROM-04: Readers expect justice. The villain gets a comeuppance that restores balance, most often arrest and punishment, though the culprit's death can also deliver it. [S2]
- MYS-PROM-05: Characters should be able to stand without the mystery. Readers should be hooked more by the characters than by the twists. [S3]

## Structure
- MYS-STR-01: One workable beat sequence is hook, inciting event (the crime occurs or the detective learns of it), first plot point where stakes turn personal, midpoint, third plot point, climax and resolution. [S3]
- MYS-STR-02: Put the culprit on the page early. The killer should be in the book from Act I, so introduce the culprit on the page by 25% of the manuscript (house setting). [S1][S2]
- MYS-STR-03: At the midpoint the biggest clue yet transforms the case, but understanding stays incomplete. [S3]
- MYS-STR-04: The third plot point is often a darker turn, such as another crime equal to or worse than the first, which also supplies the final clues. [S3]
- MYS-STR-05: Around the halfway point, a summary slipped into dialogue or a character's thoughts can reinforce the clues or red herrings you want readers to keep. [S5]
- MYS-STR-06: Readers should grasp the connections themselves before a lengthy explanation, and explaining every loose end produces a bloated whodunit scene at the end. [S1][S4]

## Conventions
- MYS-CONV-01: Whatever the narrator or sleuth sees, the reader sees too. Misdirect attention, but never withhold a clue the sleuth has found. [S1][S2]
- MYS-CONV-02: Foreshadowing has a setup (plant the clue) and a payoff (decode it), and misdirection works between them. Disguise or bury the early clue, but it must be present. [S3][S1]
- MYS-CONV-03: Clue techniques include a viewpoint character who draws a wrong conclusion, a clue placed just before a dramatic event, a wrong premise such as the time of the crime, and an absence that is itself the clue. [S5]
- MYS-CONV-04: Give every significant character a secret and vary the intensity of the secrets, from shocking to meaningful only to that character. [S4]
- MYS-CONV-05: Red herrings may mislead but should not be false. Tricking readers is not the same as lying to them, so the narration never states an untruth. [S5]
- MYS-CONV-06: Decide which red herrings need resolving. Resolve any that readers will feel owed an explanation for, and let minor ones stay open only if the plot moves fast and they prove irrelevant. [S4]
- MYS-CONV-07: The detective solves the case by wits. Do not rely on luck, coincidence or revelation from nowhere [S2], or on devices the story world has not established, such as a clone in a story that never acknowledged cloning [S4]. [S2][S4]
- MYS-CONV-08: Point of view sets the form of fair play. In single first-person or close POV the reader learns what the sleuth learns. With several third-person viewpoints readers may learn things the detective does not, but the detective should not make a major discovery the reader is denied. [S1]

## Pitfalls
- MYS-PIT-01: The sleuth has an epiphany from a clue the reader never saw. It breaks trust and can end in bad reviews. [S2][S1]
- MYS-PIT-02: Introducing the murderer late, such as the second-to-last chapter after the clues all pointed elsewhere, feels like a rabbit from a hat. [S2][S4]
- MYS-PIT-03: Some clues sit on the page yet cannot be used without specialist knowledge, such as a trace-free chemical or a foreign alphabet detail. [S5]
- MYS-PIT-04: Modern readers often spot misdirection once a detail gets more than casual emphasis. The fix is to make readers emotionally invested in the misdirection so they scrutinise less. [S3]
- MYS-PIT-05: Contrived cliffhangers and withheld reveals can feel like clickbait. Use them only when they arise naturally from the story. [S1]
- MYS-PIT-06: Too many false clues overwhelm readers or leave important ones unexplained, so track which red herrings stay, which become backstory and which get resolved. [S4]
- MYS-PIT-07: Reader dealbreakers include implausible sleuthing that no sensible person would do and killing a beloved series character without warning. Too much danger is exhausting and too much routine goes flat, so balance them. [S1]

## Reader psychology
Builds on the reader-engagement skill.
- MYS-ENG-01: Every clue the solution depends on is stated on the page, in a scene before the reveal, even if disguised among other detail. Keep a clue ledger in the notes listing each clue and the scene where it appears. [S1][S2]
  Check: for each clue named in the solution, quote the sentence in an earlier scene that shows it.
- MYS-ENG-02: When the POV character notices something significant, the text says what it is in that scene. No line says the character saw or understood something without telling the reader what it was. [S1]
  Check: search for phrases such as "couldn't believe what" or "it all made sense" and confirm the cause is stated in the same scene.
- MYS-ENG-03: The culprit is named or shown on the page before 25% of the manuscript (house setting). [S1]
  Check: find the first appearance of the culprit and confirm it falls before the 25% mark.
- MYS-ENG-04: Each answer a scene reveals raises a new question or obstacle, because suspense lives in the character's goal and not only in the solution. [S1]
  Check: after any scene that reveals something, a sentence names the next question or obstacle it creates.
- MYS-ENG-05: Before the final explanation scene, every red herring readers would feel owed an answer for has been resolved on the page. [S4]
  Check: for each herring in the ledger marked as owed, find the scene that resolves it; list any left unresolved with a reason.
- MYS-ENG-06: Chapter breaks that withhold a reveal are used only when the POV character does not already know the withheld thing, and not on every chapter. [S1]
  Check: at each cliffhanger chapter end, confirm the POV character lacks the information being withheld; the check fails if every chapter ends this way.
- MYS-ENG-07: A recap beat that restates the key clues or red herrings in dialogue or thought sits between 40% and 60% of the manuscript (house setting). [S5]
  Check: find the recap beat and confirm its position.

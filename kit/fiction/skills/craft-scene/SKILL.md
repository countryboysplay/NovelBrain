# Scene Craft

These are craft principles, not formulas. Writers and editors disagree on terms (disaster, turning point and value shift all name overlapping ideas), so treat each framework below as an option and judge a scene by whether it changes something, not by whether it matches a template. The book's audience and genre skills and style guide override this skill on content, length and voice.

## Principles

- CN-PRIN-01: A scene is a unit of change. Something at stake for the point-of-view character should stand differently at the end than at the start; Story Grid defines a scene by a value shift and says it does not end until the value has shifted. Swain and Bickham frame it as the outcome of a goal that met opposition. The change can be small or internal. [S4] [S1]
- CN-PRIN-02: A scene usually works best when the character wants something specific now, something or someone opposes it, and the result is unexpected and significant. The goal should be concrete and observable, and the stakes real: if success or failure would not change where the story goes, the scene is a candidate for cutting; a scene with no goal, conflict or hook may be filler. [S1] [S11] [S10]
- CN-PRIN-03: The outcome should be unexpected but logical. It grows out of the conflict that was shown and answers what the goal raised. Swain's disaster is not required in every scene; common shapes are a flat no, a "no, and it gets worse", or a "yes, but" where success brings a new problem, and quiet scenes may shift something smaller. [S9] [S1] [S11]
- CN-PRIN-04: A sequel is the other half of Swain's pattern: reaction, dilemma, decision. It links one scene's outcome to the next scene's goal, and readers' investment follows the character's reaction to a setback. Judge sequels by function (do they connect cause to effect and end in a choice), not by proportion. [S2] [S3]
- CN-PRIN-05: Scenes are rendered in a continuous stretch of time, with real-time movement (action, dialogue, engagement with people and place) outweighing reflection and exposition. Heighten reality and cut the mundane. [S5]
- CN-PRIN-06: The more important the moment, the stronger the case for dramatising it. Major turning points and the scenes a genre promises readers belong on the page; routine passages, time passing and known information can be summarised. [S7] [S5]
- CN-PRIN-07: Scene frameworks are lenses, not laws. Swain's pattern, Bickham's scene question and Story Grid's five commandments overlap and are taught with different terms, and Swain allowed that parts can be omitted, reordered or merged. Use whichever helps find the change in the scene. [S1] [S2] [S4]

## Techniques

- CN-TECH-01: State the scene goal early, usually near the top of the scene, in words or action the reader can see. Teasers and deliberate mystery are the exceptions. An abstract aim (proving love) should appear as a tangible action. [S1]
- CN-TECH-02: Escalate the conflict as a moving chain of stimulus and response rather than repeating one argument. Opposition can be direct, such as an enemy, or indirect, such as interruptions and diversions, and it tests how much the character wants the goal. [S1] [S11]
- CN-TECH-03: Choose the outcome type on purpose: direct block, indirect block (diversion), partial block, or hollow victory where success does harm. Match its size to genre and character; a small or internal setback can work if it pushes the plot and prompts a new goal. [S9]
- CN-TECH-04: Enter late and leave early. Treat a scene as a slice from the middle of a chain of events: begin just before the action starts, skip routine steps and pleasantries the reader can infer, and stop when the scene's purpose is achieved or on a peak or revelation, before characters wind down and restate what the reader knows. [S8] [S5]
- CN-TECH-05: End a scene on a high point (a small victory) or a low point (cliff-hanging suspense or uncertainty). The source says the choice matters less than that the ending sets up future conflict and creates consequences the next scene must deal with, and that readers should not be left too satisfied. [S5] [S1]
- CN-TECH-06: Use summary as connective tissue. Sequential summary compresses a specific span (a day, a week) in order; circumstantial summary shows how things usually went. To skip time, jump past the dull stretch, often further than an hour or two, and mark the gap with white space or a visible symbol so it survives page breaks and ebook formatting. [S6]
- CN-TECH-07: Blend modes when summary must carry weight: a half-scene gives one significant action a little detail before returning to summary, and a snippet of real-time dialogue makes a long summarised stretch feel less abrupt. [S7]
- CN-TECH-08: Vary sequel length to control pace. Short sequels, even a sentence or two of recommitment, suit urgent stretches; longer ones suit major turning points and slow the pace. Introspection and backstory sit more comfortably in sequels than in action scenes. [S3] [S2]

## Pitfalls

- CN-PIT-01: The unchanging scene: events happen, but the value, goal or situation ends where it started. Dramatising material that deserved only summary produces a flat scene that hurts pace. [S4] [S7]
- CN-PIT-02: Intensity mistaken for turn. A scene can get louder or change location without anything at stake changing; Story Grid says chapter or location breaks are not a consistent definition of a scene and that a scene turns on its value shift. Check the shift, not the volume. [S4]
- CN-PIT-03: A goal with no consequences, or no visible goal, leaves the reader unable to tell progress from setback. [S1]
- CN-PIT-04: Conflict that circles without developing, or a disaster that is random rather than logical. Disasters that do not fit the story's context read as melodrama, and a fake-out disaster can feel like an authorial lie. [S11] [S9]
- CN-PIT-05: Missing or bloated sequels. A book that feels breathless may be missing reaction and decision; a book that drags may be sitting in sequels that are too long or that end without a decision, which leaves the next scene aimless. [S3] [S2]
- CN-PIT-06: Starting too early or ending too late: preamble, greetings, travel and wrap-up chatter the reader can fill in. Cutting an overlong build-up or tedious falling action is among the most common edits. [S8] [S5]
- CN-PIT-07: Summarising a high-impact moment, such as a climax or an obligatory genre scene, can leave readers feeling cheated. Summarising out of fear of writing the scene is a drafting problem; draft it badly and revise. [S7]
- CN-PIT-08: A scene that is a lecture, a long stretch of backstory, or a detour unrelated to the protagonist's goals. [S5]

## Checks

- CN-CHK-01: Every scene changes something. [S4] [S1]
  Check: Pass if the scene text or outline entry names a before and an after for at least one thing at stake (a value, a relationship, what a character knows, wants, fears or believes) and the two differ, even slightly. Fail if the after matches the before or no stake is named.
- CN-CHK-02: Every scene has a visible goal with something at stake. [S1] [S11]
  Check: Pass if within the scene a point-of-view character pursues a concrete, observable aim (in a reaction scene the aim can be to decide, recover or understand) and the text or outline says what is lost or gained if it fails. Fail if no aim can be identified or failing would change nothing later.
- CN-CHK-03: Opposition is present and develops. [S1] [S11]
  Check: Pass if something or someone blocks the goal (an enemy, an interruption, circumstance or the character's own doubt) and the obstacle changes or escalates during the scene. Fail if there is no resistance, or the same obstacle repeats unchanged.
- CN-CHK-04: The outcome follows from the conflict and is not a clean yes. [S9] [S1]
  Check: Pass if the scene ends with a failure, a partial result, or a success that brings a new cost, problem or question, and that result grows out of events already shown. Fail if the goal is met with no cost and no new question, or the result comes from an unconnected random event, unless the outline marks the scene as a deliberate breather and says what it prepares.
- CN-CHK-05: The outcome hands something to the next scene. [S9] [S2]
  Check: Pass if the next scene or sequel opens with the character facing the consequence, or the outline names the new goal or open problem this outcome creates. Fail if the next scene could be read first with no loss.
- CN-CHK-06: Important moments are on the page. [S7] [S5]
  Check: Pass if each major turning point and each scene the genre or outline promises is rendered as continuous scene text with action, dialogue and place. Fail if one is delivered only as summary or reported afterward.
- CN-CHK-07: Scenes enter late and leave early. [S8] [S5]
  Check: Pass if the first lines are already in the action or the scene problem, and the last lines fall on a decision, revelation, setback or open question rather than goodbyes or a restatement of what the reader knows. Fail if the first paragraph is routine setup (waking, travelling, greetings) or the last paragraph recaps the scene. Leaving early is allowed; leaving without a closing shift is not (CN-CHK-10).
- CN-CHK-08: Reaction and decision exist where a setback needs one. [S2] [S3]
  Check: Pass if after a major setback the character reacts and reaches a choice that sets the next goal, even if brief or by summary. Fail if the next goal appears with no reaction or motive after a major setback, or the sequel ends with no decision.
- CN-CHK-09: Time gaps and summary are signposted and justified. [S6] [S7]
  Check: Pass if every jump in time or place is marked (white space, symbol, orienting line) and the summarised material is routine, transitional or already known. Fail if a gap leaves the reader unable to tell when or where the next scene is, or if summary covers a moment with conflict and consequence.
- CN-CHK-10: The scene ends on a closed beat (its outcome lands on the page) or on a deliberate hand-off the next scene picks up at once. A scene that stops mid-action, with its situation unresolved and the next scene starting somewhere else, fails. [S9] [S2]
  Check: Pass if the last lines land the scene's outcome or hand off on purpose to the scene that follows at once. Fail if the scene stops mid-action with its situation unresolved and the next scene starts elsewhere.

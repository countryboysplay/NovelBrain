# Young adult (about ages 12-18)

Young adult (YA) is a category, not a genre. Genre skills layer on top of this profile. Teens read up, so the protagonist is usually the reader's age or a little older.

Gaps in the evidence: no reliable source found covers teen readers' own views on "adult voice" beyond the teen-written criteria in S1, a cadence for romance tension, or hard prevalence data for first-person present tense (S9 only confirms a trend). S13 was read at abstract level only, so it supports only what its abstract reports. S11 is UK guidance on adult literature that this profile applies to YA because it says young people are more susceptible. No source states a YA rule on drugs and alcohol, so YA-CON-08 is a house setting. Numbers marked "(house setting)" are this kit's operating choices, tagged with the closest source for their direction, and are not figures any source states.

## Length
- YA-LEN-01: Contemporary and realistic YA runs about 50,000-75,000 words; 55,000-79,999 is a safe core range for most YA, and below about 47,000 is risky. [S3][S4][S7]
- YA-LEN-02: Fantasy and science fiction may run longer for worldbuilding, to about 85,000 words in editorial guidance and up to about 100,000 in agent word-count lists. [S4][S5][S6][S7]
- YA-LEN-03: A YA manuscript over 90,000 words needs a stated reason such as complex worldbuilding or a dual timeline. [S4][S6]
- YA-LEN-04: Chapters run roughly 1,000-3,500 words per one YA novelist, and contemporary YA averages nearer 1,500-2,000 per a contributing author; both are practitioner estimates, not publisher limits. Vary chapter length on purpose, with an occasional very short chapter for impact. [S10]
- YA-LEN-05: Younger YA (protagonist 14-15, content aimed at readers moving up from middle grade) sits at the low end of the length range. Do not pad it toward the top. [S3][S7]

## Language
- YA-LANG-01: Never simplify the language, story or style to talk down to teens. Write at least as well as you would for adults. [S2][S1]
- YA-LANG-02: Narration uses common, accessible vocabulary and short, direct exchanges. Teens in fiction do not deliver long monologues. Cut adult phrasing that the protagonist would not use. [S2][S7]
- YA-LANG-03: Slang is sparse and dated terms are avoided. Do not add swearing, sex or "cool" language only to seem current. [S7][S8]
- YA-LANG-04: Voice lives in word choice, sentence and paragraph length, syntax, punctuation and cadence, across the whole page and not only in dialogue. Do not default to snark; match the voice to the character. [S2]
- YA-LANG-05: Quantitative readability scores are a screening aid only. School text bands put grades 6-8 at Flesch-Kincaid 6.51-10.34 and grades 9-10 at 8.32-12.12, and fiction scores are less reliable than informational text. Judge by qualitative reading when a score looks off. [S12]

## POV, tense and protagonist
- YA-POV-01: The protagonist is a teen, usually 14-18. A 12-year-old lead reads as middle grade; a college-age lead reads as new adult. [S2][S3][S6][S7]
- YA-POV-02: Younger YA uses protagonists aged 14-15 with cleaner content. For the widest YA audience use 17 or 18, but not already at college. [S3][S7]
- YA-POV-03: Narration is first person or close third, told in the moment or from the recent past. It is not an adult looking back on adolescence. [S2][S3][S8]
- YA-POV-04: Present tense is common in YA, especially first person present, but tense matters less than whether the book works. Choose it for immediacy and keep it consistent. Do not use it to hide thin story. [S9]
- YA-POV-05: The story is driven by the teen protagonist's own dramatized choices and actions. Adult characters support the story and do not take it over. A teen protagonist has peers around them. [S2][S8]
- YA-POV-06: State the protagonist's age early in the text, because content expectations shift between a 14-year-old and an 18-year-old. [S6]

## Content limits
- YA-CON-01: Profanity, violence, romance and sexuality are all allowable in YA, but each must serve the story and never be gratuitous or merely edgy. [S2][S3][S7]
- YA-CON-02: Sex is not eroticised. Sexual content stays non-graphic: kissing, implication, fade to black or brief non-explicit summary. Violence is not overly graphic. [S3][S7]
- YA-CON-03: Content scales with protagonist age. Protagonists of 14-15 get cleaner language and romance. Heavier language and heavier content belong with 16-18, and heavy profanity can cost school and library support. [S3][S6][S7]
- YA-CON-04: Hard topics are open to YA, including suicide, eating disorders, assault and police violence. Handle them honestly and with purpose; do not sugar-coat social issues. [S1][S2][S8]
- YA-CON-05: When depicting suicide or self-harm, give as little detail on method as possible: name no drug, dose or tool, and do not describe how the means were obtained. Do not invent a new or unusual method. [S11]
- YA-CON-06: Never present suicide or self-harm as easy, quick, peaceful, painless, inevitable, a release, or a source of reward. Show complexity, not a single cause, and do not show a quick return to normal after an attempt. [S11]
- YA-CON-07: Where a character shows mastery over a crisis, show it by seeking help and coming through it. Use the plain terms "died by suicide" or "attempted suicide," not "commit suicide" or "successful attempt." [S11]

- YA-CON-08: Drug and alcohol use is allowed when it serves the story, is shown with consequences and no glamour, and gets more restraint for protagonists aged 14-15 (house setting). [S2][S7]
- YA-CON-09: Flag for human review at the act checkpoint any content beyond these defaults (graphic sex or violence, method detail for self-harm, glamorised substance use) and any protagonist aged 12-13 (house setting; S3 and S7 support only the age bands, not the review flag, and such a lead may belong in middle grade). [S3][S7]

## Pacing and themes
- YA-PACE-01: YA moves faster than adult fiction. Build momentum with tension, conflict and points of no return, and avoid long static description. [S8]
- YA-PACE-02: Plots are rich, usually with several subplots running beside a busy main plot. [S8]
- YA-PACE-03: Core themes are identity, shifting relationships, first love and first experiences, loss of innocence, and autonomy: how the hero fits into the world beyond friends and family. [S2][S3]
- YA-PACE-04: Endings can be unresolved, downbeat or ambiguous, and need not be happy, but the ending must feel satisfying. [S1][S3][S7]
- YA-PACE-05: Plot events have causes. An event that happens only because the author wants it to is a defect. [S2]
- YA-PACE-06: The lesson is never preached. The story follows the protagonist toward emotional truth and lets the reader draw the conclusion. [S2][S8]
- YA-PACE-07: Characters are distinct, believable and consistent, with no stock stereotypes such as the dumb jock. The cast stands apart from other teen titles. [S1][S2]

## Reader psychology
Builds on the reader-engagement skill.

- YA-ENG-01: Narration stays inside the teen's present perception. No sentence in the narration offers adult hindsight about the protagonist's teen years. [S3][S8]
  Check: search the narration (outside the teen's own thoughts and dialogue) for retrospective frames such as "years later," "looking back," "I would learn," "little did I know" (phrase list is a house setting); any hit that gives adult hindsight fails.
- YA-ENG-02: Every chapter ends on a hook, which can be emotional as well as physical: an unanswered question, a reversal, a decision pending or a point of no return. [S8][S10]
  Check: the last 150 words (house setting) of the chapter leave a specific question open or a choice pending; a closing summary, reflection or peaceful wrap-up fails.
- YA-ENG-03: Emotion arrives through action, sensory detail and the character's own reaction, not through the narrator stating the feeling. [S8]
  Check: in the chapter's three highest-emotion moments (house setting), count lines where the narrator names the emotion in the abstract and offers no physical or behavioural beat; more than one per moment (house setting) fails.
- YA-ENG-04: No narrator or adult character delivers a moral. The protagonist's choice and its cost show the point. [S2][S1][S8]
  Check: find any passage of two or more sentences (house setting) in which a narrator or adult states what the protagonist "should" learn or do; any such passage that no teen character disputes or ignores fails.
- YA-ENG-05: The protagonist's problem is treated as serious. No narrator, adult or the protagonist's own reflection dismisses a feeling as hormones, a phase or overreaction without the story proving that framing wrong. [S1][S8]
  Check: search for dismissal phrases ("just hormones," "a phase," "overreacting," "teenage drama"; phrase list is a house setting) in narration or adult dialogue; each hit must be contested in the same scene or it fails.
- YA-ENG-06: Social stakes are on the page in every chapter (house setting; S3 supports only that YA heroes find their place in the world beyond friends and family, and S2 that peers and relationships matter): each chapter shows how an event changes the protagonist's standing, friendships, family relationships or sense of self among peers. [S3][S2]
  Check: name the relationship or standing that is at risk or changes in the chapter; if none can be named, the chapter fails.
- YA-ENG-07: Romance tension is kept alive by obstacles and withheld payoff, with a physical or emotional step forward or a new block (house setting: no source gives a cadence). [S8]
  Check: in a book with a romance, list the beats where the pair's status changes or is blocked; no more than five consecutive chapters (house setting) pass without one.
- YA-ENG-08: Teens are given the ordinary tools of voice, not tropes: narration shows the protagonist's opinions on small things, and sarcasm is not the default tone. [S2][S7]
  Check: in a 1,000-word sample of narration (house setting), at least two opinions or asides (house setting) are specific to this character, and fewer than half of the character's lines (house setting) rely on sarcasm.
- YA-ENG-09: Cut any set-up that does not move plot or character. [S8][S10]
  Check: for each scene or chapter, state in one line what changes (plot fact, relationship or the protagonist's belief); a chapter with no change fails.

## Put-down risks
- YA-RISK-01: Talking down to the reader, through simplified language, explained jokes or embedded lessons, is the largest risk. Teens notice writing that tries too hard to sound cool. [S1][S7][S8]
- YA-RISK-02: An adult-sounding narrator on a teen character, wiser and calmer than a teenager would be, breaks the voice. Check vocabulary and maturity line by line. [S3][S2]
- YA-RISK-03: Long description with no tension, such as pages of setting or backstory, slows YA past the point readers tolerate. [S8]
- YA-RISK-04: Stereotyped characters and plot beats copied from bestsellers read as imitation. Do not chase trends. [S2][S1]
- YA-RISK-05: Content used to look edgy, including gratuitous sex, violence or swearing, loses teen readers and loses gatekeeper support. [S3][S7]
- YA-RISK-06: A cover, summary or opening that promises one story and delivers another (a bait and switch) loses teen readers. [S1]

## Re-engagement
- YA-RE-01: After a slow reflective chapter, the next chapter opens with a concrete event, decision or new problem within its first 150 words (house setting). [S8]
- YA-RE-02: Vary chapter length: an occasional very short or very long chapter adds effect, and moderate chapters give readers natural places to rest. After a run of quick chapters, give the next chapter room for characters to react (house setting). [S10]
- YA-RE-03: When a hard-topic scene has landed, show the protagonist's help-seeking, coping or support in the following scenes. Do not leave the reader alone with the crisis. [S11]
- YA-RE-04: Keep a thread of choice and agency alive: when the protagonist is stuck, the next scene gives them a decision, not a rescue by an adult (house setting). [S3]
- YA-RE-05: Show the protagonist's sense of self and relationships shifting over the book (house setting). In one study of eighth-graders reading self-chosen YA, students reported changes in their identity, agency and relationships as outcomes of engaged reading; the abstract does not say what text features cause this. [S13]

# Character Craft

These are craft principles drawn from working editors and writing teachers, not formulas. Competing frameworks are listed as options. The book's audience skill, genre skills and style guide override them on content, length and voice. A failed check is a flag for judgement, not an automatic rejection.

## Principles
- CC-PRIN-01: The protagonist has a conscious want that drives the plot and an inner need they do not yet see. One common framework treats wants as the plot and needs as the character arc. Weiland's version ties the want to a false belief (the Lie) and the need to the matching Truth. These are options, not the only model. [S3][S4]
- CC-PRIN-02: A protagonist needs a specific flaw or false belief to move away from. A flawless lead has no room to change, and the flaw should be shown in the opening so its cost is visible. Pick one imperfection to anchor the arc instead of many. [S1][S2]
- CC-PRIN-03: Choose an arc type on purpose. Three common options are the positive arc (the character drops the Lie and accepts the Truth), the flat arc (the character already holds the Truth and changes others while keeping conviction under doubt), and the negative arc (the character ends worse off, often by clinging to the Lie). Other arc models exist. [S2][S3]
- CC-PRIN-04: The protagonist drives the plot. Events should follow from what the protagonist chooses, not only happen to them. Reacting is not automatically passive when the reaction is a chosen, effective response. [S5]
- CC-PRIN-05: Change and consistency are shown through choices under pressure. Plot events should press the protagonist toward the arc, and the outcome shows whether they changed or held firm. [S1][S2]
- CC-PRIN-06: Every character who matters has wants of their own. Allies, love interests and antagonists pursue their own goals with their own logic, and those goals meet the protagonist's as help or friction. [S7][S6]
- CC-PRIN-07: Each speaking character has a recognisable voice that comes from who they are: upbringing, education, career, worldview and generation. Voice also covers how they see the world and what they say to themselves, and it stays consistent. [S10][S11]
- CC-PRIN-08: Introduce characters in small groups with the spotlight on the protagonist. Cast size has no fixed rule. It is as large as the story's scope needs and no larger. [S8][S9]

## Techniques
- CC-TECH-01: State the protagonist's want, need and Lie in one sentence each before drafting. Add the concrete past event or backstory that explains the flaw, and spread it through the book in small amounts instead of one block. [S1][S3]
- CC-TECH-02: Plan how pursuing the want puts the protagonist into situations that expose the need. List which characters support the growth and which reflect the negative path. Record the steps of change across the plot. [S4]
- CC-TECH-03: For a flat arc, give the protagonist a Truth to defend and a world or supporting character who changes in response. For a negative arc, plan the repeated chances the character has to turn back and refuses. [S2][S3]
- CC-TECH-04: Give the protagonist a long-term goal and a goal in each scene. Where the plot hands the protagonist something, try turning "happens to" into "happens because of" a protagonist choice. [S5]
- CC-TECH-05: Make a voice guide for each speaking character with a few sample lines. Make a dialogue folder after drafting, with each character's lines in one place, and read it for inconsistencies, repeated phrases and sentence habits. Edit one character's dialogue at a time. [S10][S11]
- CC-TECH-06: Write each secondary character's own goal and what is behind it. Review each scene from their viewpoint to see what they want in it. Make help cost the protagonist something. Give each ally a different inner motivation, so no two allies want the same thing for the same reason. [S7]
- CC-TECH-07: Give a major adversary a reason: something to prove, revenge, or a threatened belief or identity. Choose the adversary type deliberately (competitor, rival, antagonist, force, villain and others). [S6]
- CC-TECH-08: When bringing in a new character, tie them to the protagonist or to what the protagonist is doing, give a memorable trait or mannerism where it fits, and leave unneeded extras unnamed. Choose names that differ in first letter and sound. [S8][S9]

## Pitfalls
- CC-PIT-01: A protagonist who only witnesses, stands near, receives from others, or endures events. Test: would the events unfold the same if this character were absent? [S5]
- CC-PIT-02: An arc that is announced but never tested. The flaw is named, then plot events do not press on it, so the ending change is unearned. Extra plot points that do not serve the arc may need to be cut. [S1]
- CC-PIT-03: A want that is the same as the need, or a Lie that is never shown affecting a choice, so there is no inner conflict. [S3][S4]
- CC-PIT-04: Secondary characters who exist only to help or to block, or antagonists who oppose the protagonist for no logical reason. [S7][S6]
- CC-PIT-05: Every character sounds like the author, or like each other. In two-person scenes the weaker voice can disappear while the stronger one sounds idealised. Exaggerated dialect or tics are a poor substitute for a real voice. [S10][S11]
- CC-PIT-06: A crowded introduction. Opening with a dozen characters, mostly names, is a sign of backstory loading and overwhelms the reader. [S9][S8]
- CC-PIT-07: Secondary dialogue that only delivers world information or plot facts, with no emotion or intent of its own. [S7]
- CC-PIT-08: Characters with no story purpose kept in the cast. Cut them, or check whether one character can fill several roles. [S8]

## Checks
- CC-CHK-01: The protagonist has a stated want, a stated need and a stated flaw or false belief in the outline or character record, and the want and need differ. [S3][S4]
  Check: quote the want and the need from the outline or character record, plus the flaw or false belief unless the outline marks a flat arc (then quote the Truth the protagonist already holds). Fail if any is missing, or if want and need are the same sentence in different words and the outline gives no reason (the sources describe want and need as usually in tension, though they can align by the end).
- CC-CHK-02: The flaw shows up in the protagonist's early choices, with a visible cost. [S1]
  Check: name one early scene where the protagonist's choice or behaviour shows the flaw and state the cost in the text; fail if none can be named, unless the outline marks a flat arc.
- CC-CHK-03: The outline states the arc type (positive, flat, negative or another named model), and the ending matches it. [S2][S3]
  Check: write the arc type and the protagonist's starting and ending belief; fail if the type is not stated, or if the ending contradicts the stated type without a recorded reason.
- CC-CHK-04: The protagonist's choices cause at least one major event in each act or section. [S5]
  Check: list the major plot events in the section and mark each as caused by a protagonist choice or caused by something else; fail if none is protagonist-caused.
- CC-CHK-05: In the scene, the protagonist makes a choice under pressure that shows consistency or change. [S1][S2]
  Check: quote the choice and the pressure on it; fail if the scene contains no protagonist choice with a stated cost or alternative, unless the plan marks the scene as a deliberate interlude.
- CC-CHK-06: Each recurring secondary character and each major adversary has a stated goal of their own in the cast notes or shown in the text. [S7][S6]
  Check: for each recurring named character list their goal from the notes or the text; flag each with no goal. Fail if a major adversary has no goal of their own. Treat the others as flags for judgement (house setting: no threshold, since the sources say only that each character has wants of their own).
- CC-CHK-07: Each speaking character's dialogue differs from the others in at least one nameable way, and stays consistent across scenes. [S10][S11]
  Check: for two speaking characters in the scene, state one way each speaks differently (sentence length, vocabulary, habit); fail if none can be stated for either, and flag any line that contradicts the voice guide.
- CC-CHK-08: Few new characters are introduced at once, and the focus stays on the protagonist. [S8][S9]
  Check: count new named characters in the passage and name the focus character; flag for review if several new characters arrive with no way for the reader to tell them apart or tie them to the protagonist. The sources give "two or maybe three" characters a page [S8] and "one or two at a time" in chapter one [S9] as rules of thumb; house setting: flag when more than three are introduced in one scene, unless the plan allows an ensemble or crowd scene.
- CC-CHK-09: Characters kept in the cast have a role the story uses. [S8]
  Check: for each named character state what they do that the plot or arc uses; flag any who do nothing, and mark the flag resolved if the plan records a reason.

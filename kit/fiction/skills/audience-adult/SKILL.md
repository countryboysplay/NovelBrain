# Adult (general adult fiction)

## Length
- AD-LEN-01: For a debut in literary, mainstream, women's, romance, mystery, suspense, thriller or horror, aim for 80,000-89,999 words; 90,000-99,999 is generally safe, and 70,000-79,999 or 100,000-109,999 is probably fine. [S2]
- AD-LEN-02: Under 70,000 words reads as too short and 110,000 or more reads as too long for those genres. Plan to be the rule, not the exception. [S2]
- AD-LEN-03: Science fiction and fantasy run longer because of description and world-building: 100,000-115,000 words is the best range, and 90,000-100,000 or 115,000-124,000 is probably acceptable. [S2]
- AD-LEN-04: Lighter women's fiction can sit at 70,000-75,000 words, and westerns at 50,000-80,000 with about 65,000 as a good goal. [S2]
- AD-LEN-05: Cost drives the upper limit: books past 100,000 words cost more to produce, which is why agents and editors resist them. A manuscript that runs long usually needs cutting, so cut redundancy before adding length. [S2]
- AD-LEN-06: Chapters run 1,500-4,500 words. Literary fiction, romance and women's fiction run 2,000-4,000; crime and thrillers 1,000-3,000; fantasy and epic historical fiction 3,000-5,000. [S8]
- AD-LEN-07: An average scene runs 1,000-2,000 words. Scenes under 1,000 words are short, and leaning on scenes under 500 words risks reading like head-hopping, except in thrillers and similar fast books that deliberately use very short scene-chapters. [S10]

## Language
- AD-LANG-01: Build a distinct, assured voice that is clear first. Clarity and simplicity cut through, and a distinctive voice does not excuse confusion. [S1][S3]
- AD-LANG-02: Limit similes, metaphors and descriptors. Overwriting from pressure to impress is a leading agent complaint and reads as lack of confidence. [S1]
- AD-LANG-03: Use description sparingly so every sentence earns its place. Descriptions should be unexpected, precise and intentional, not generic. [S1]
- AD-LANG-04: Replace vague wording with precise, concrete detail. [S3]
- AD-LANG-05: Show information through action and gradual reveal rather than telling it outright. [S1]
- AD-LANG-06: Pitch the prose so a reader can process it smoothly. In the study, self-reported reading flow was highest when readers felt their skill fit the book's challenge, so avoid prose that is far harder than the genre norm. [S5]
Not covered by the sources: sentence length, paragraph length and a reading-grade target for adult fiction. No numeric limits are set here; follow the genre skill and the author's voice.

## POV, tense and protagonist
- AD-POV-01: Any POV and tense is allowed if it works, meaning the story holds the reader's attention and draws them on. Choose the device to serve the story, not to be unusual. [S7]
- AD-POV-02: If tense or POV changes between chapters or strands (for example a first-person present criminal and a first-person past detective), the change must serve the story. A tense shift alone is not a reason a reader stops. [S7]
- AD-POV-03: The opening must orient a stranger: where we are, whose viewpoint we are in, and the tone. [S3]
- AD-POV-04: The protagonist must be compelling, not necessarily likeable, and the reader should care about them early. Without a character to connect with, readers feel like outsiders. [S1][S4]
Not covered by the sources: a typical protagonist age for adult fiction. No age rule is set; the premise decides.
- AD-POV-05: Keep the opening focused on the protagonist's thoughts or actions near the inciting incident. A character's thoughts can place the reader there without dramatic action. [S1]

## Content limits
- AD-CON-01: Adult fiction has no age-based content ceiling. Genre and the writer's choice set what appears, and the brief's book.yaml rating (if any) overrides this default. [S9]
- AD-CON-02: Flag content that is intense or sensitive (sexual violence, abuse, suicide or self-harm, graphic gore, bigotry) and explicit sex scenes in a note to the reader. [S9]
- AD-CON-03: Word content notes by naming the category specifically, such as "child abuse" rather than "abuse", and without naming plot twists. [S9]
- AD-CON-04: Place content notes in the front pages, or in the back matter with a front note saying where they are. [S9]
- AD-CON-05: Match tone, content and focus to what the premise and blurb promise. Abrupt changes in focus, tone, pacing, POV or era betray the promise. [S4]

## Pacing and themes
- AD-PACE-01: Fast action is not required. The real pacing risk is drift, and a slow start is not rescued later. [S3]
- AD-PACE-02: Start each scene as late as possible and end it as early as possible. Save backstory for later. [S1]
- AD-PACE-03: Balance exposition and backstory with scenes that move the plot. Too much info-dumping stalls the story. [S4]
- AD-PACE-04: Vary chapter length to control pace: short chapters for tense or action-heavy stretches, longer ones for reflective passages. [S8]
- AD-PACE-05: Break chapters at a change of setting, character or POV, a move between main plot and subplot, a jump in time, or a high-tension moment. [S8]
- AD-PACE-06: Deliver the genre resolution readers expect. A broken trope kills interest fast, so bend conventions only on purpose. [S4]
Not covered by the sources: how often a subplot must reappear. The only sourced guidance is to mark a move between main plot and subplot with a chapter break (AD-PACE-05) and to keep subplots from shifting the book's focus away from its promise (AD-ENG-08). Any return interval is a house setting chosen at planning time.

## Reader psychology
Builds on the reader-engagement skill.
- AD-ENG-01: The first page orients and pulls. A reader can tell where and when the scene is, whose viewpoint it is and what tone it has, and it raises a question about character, place or event. The 250-word window is a house setting. [S1][S3]
  Check: within the first 250 words (house setting) the text names a viewpoint character, gives a place or time cue, and poses one unanswered question or disturbance.
- AD-ENG-02: Do not open with waking up, a dream, musing, remembering childhood, a mirror description, or a weather or philosophical preamble. [S1][S3]
  Check: the first paragraph is not a waking, dream, mirror, weather, childhood-memory or abstract-musing passage.
- AD-ENG-03: Something happens on page one, and by the end of the first five pages the text shows a forming problem, a clarifying want or a disturbance. The 1,250-word conversion (five pages at 250 words a page) is a house setting. [S1][S3]
  Check: within the first 1,250 words (house setting) a named character wants something, meets a problem or is disturbed by an event, and the scene is not description or backstory alone.
- AD-ENG-04: Hold backstory back. Do not set up characters one by one with their histories before the story starts. The three-sentence limit is a house setting. [S1][S3]
  Check: no run of more than three consecutive sentences (house setting) of past events appears in chapter 1 before the main problem has appeared on the page.
- AD-ENG-05: Chapter 1 is not a complete short story. It ends on a complication, raised stakes or a commitment to act. The 150-word window is a house setting. [S3]
  Check: the last 150 words (house setting) of chapter 1 leave a complication unresolved, raise the stakes, or show the character committing to an action.
- AD-ENG-06: Keep stakes alive. Hold one big, emotionally charged question open until the end and tease its resolution. [S4]
  Check: for each chapter, a reviewer can quote one sentence stating the open story question or what the protagonist stands to lose, and the question is not fully answered before the final act.
- AD-ENG-07: Anticipation is built before the payoff. Before a major event scene, an earlier passage names the character's emotional stake in it. Suspense in the study means anticipating emotionally charged events. [S5]
  Check: for each major event scene, a quoted earlier line names what the viewpoint character hopes for or fears from it.
- AD-ENG-08: The book reads as advertised. Chapter 1 sets the tone, POV, era and focus that later chapters keep. [S4]
  Check: any later change of POV style, tone, era or central focus is previewed in chapter 1, or the reviewer fails it.
- AD-ENG-09: Every scene moves the story, balancing exposition against scenes that move the plot. The two-scene limit is a house setting. [S4][S1]
  Check: each scene can be summarised as one change in situation, knowledge, decision or relationship, and no two consecutive scenes (house setting) lack such a change.
- AD-ENG-10: Early in the book the reader should know what the protagonist wants and why to care what happens next. Agents look for a forming problem or clarifying want within the first five pages (see AD-ENG-03), and one agent says she decides around page 50 whether she cares what happens next. The 12,500-word mark (page 50 at 250 words a page) and the test items are house settings. [S7][S3]
  Check: by 12,500 words (house setting) the protagonist's goal is stated in text and at least one obstacle to it has appeared on the page.

## Put-down risks
- AD-RISK-01: Boredom from a slow, inert start is a common reason readers quit, and pages that are pleasant but slow get polite agent rejections. [S3][S4]
- AD-RISK-02: No character connection. Give the protagonist coping, wants and secondary figures worth caring about. [S4]
- AD-RISK-03: Low or fading stakes. Test: the protagonist's stated stake in the final third is quotable and at least as large as in chapter 1 (house setting). [S4]
- AD-RISK-04: Bait-and-switch, where focus, tone, pacing, POV or era changes against the promise. Test: the chapter 1 tone, POV and era match the blurb and every later chapter (house setting). [S4]
- AD-RISK-05: Doldrums from too much exposition, backstory or info-dumping. Test: no chapter is mostly exposition or backstory with no plot-moving scene (house setting). [S4]
- AD-RISK-06: Excess length: cut ruthlessly, remove redundancy, and trust the reader. [S4][S2]
- AD-RISK-07: Violated genre expectations. Test: the ending delivers the resolution named in the genre skill's reader promise, or the departure is stated as deliberate in the plan (house setting). [S4]
- AD-RISK-08: A writer who seems unsure of the route: a shocking line that goes nowhere, or a book that seems to be "starting soon". [S1][S3]

## Re-engagement
- AD-RE-01: Quitting is easy and common, so end each chapter on a pull: a complication, raised stakes or commitment to act. About 20 percent of books Goodreads members read go unfinished (a single 2013 figure, S6). [S3][S6]
- AD-RE-02: Keep chapters within the 1,500-4,500 word norm so a stopping point arrives soon. The source warns that a reader who puts the book down mid-chapter may not pick it up again. [S8]
- AD-RE-03: When a chapter follows a POV, setting or time jump, its opening lines re-orient the reader with whose viewpoint, where and when. [S3][S8]
- AD-RE-04: After a reflective stretch, return to a live question. Building the story's mental model is where reading flow comes from, and flow correlated with motivation to keep reading. [S5]
- AD-RE-05: Use shorter chapters to speed tense stretches and bring the reader back into momentum. [S8]

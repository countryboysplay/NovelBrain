# Craft: Revision

These are craft principles, not formulas. The book's audience and genre skills and its style guide override them on content, length and voice. Editorial vocabulary varies between editors and publishers, so the stages below are a common arrangement, not the only one. The rules ask what each pass is for and whether a change fixes the underlying problem. They do not ask whether a fixed number of passes was run.

## Principles
- CR-PRIN-01: Revise from the largest problem to the smallest. The common order is developmental (structure, plot, character, pacing), then line (style, sense, flow), then copyedit (grammar, usage, consistency), then proofread (final errors). Each pass assumes the one before it is finished. [S1] [S3] [S4]
- CR-PRIN-02: Do not polish text that may be cut or moved. Polishing a chapter that a structural change later removes is wasted work, so settle structure and scene purpose before sentence-level work. [S4] [S6]
- CR-PRIN-03: Keep the passes separate. A developmental pass looks at the whole story and does not fix commas. A line edit is a sentence-by-sentence edit that need not catch every small point of grammar; a copyedit checks spelling, grammar, punctuation, names and timeline logic and is not the place to rework plot or pacing. A proofread is not a rewrite. [S1] [S2] [S3] [S5] [S10]
- CR-PRIN-04: Editor notes describe a reader's experience of a problem. The suggested fix is one interpretation. Find the underlying problem, then solve it in the way that suits this book. [S6] [S7] [S9]
- CR-PRIN-05: Triage before acting. Separate big-picture issues, repeated notes (repetition signals importance), and optional or taste-based notes ("you might consider"). Treat taste notes as suggestions, not defects. [S7] [S6]
- CR-PRIN-06: Revision should make the writing sound more like the book, only clearer, stronger and more intentional. It should not sand it toward a generic idea of correct. [S9]
- CR-PRIN-07: Plan big changes where they are cheap. Revise a scene list or outline first, then redraft prose to match it. Changing a few lines of plan costs less than reworking tens of thousands of words. [S8]

## Techniques
- CR-TECH-01: Developmental pass: write a short revision letter or issue list about structure, plot, character, viewpoint and pacing, with no inline line edits. A developmental editor typically delivers a critique, not marked-up sentences. [S1] [S3] [S4]
- CR-TECH-02: Line pass: check clarity, word choice that fits each character's voice, cliches, dialogue, sentence rhythm, repetition, overwriting and tense consistency. Line editing improves language and style; a thorough line edit works best as its own step before the copyedit; it is not the stage for fixing plot. [S1] [S2] [S3] [S5]
- CR-TECH-03: Copyedit pass: keep a style sheet. Check spelling, grammar, punctuation, name spelling, dialogue tag punctuation, and timeline, setting and trait logic. Query factual and continuity slips (a character using something before it existed, a winter coat in a summer scene). [S1] [S3] [S5]
- CR-TECH-04: Proof pass: read the near-final text, ideally on the finished layout, for literal errors such as misspelled names, doubled or missing words, and layout faults. Prefer a reader other than the writer or the editor. [S3] [S5] [S10]
- CR-TECH-05: Sort each note into three piles: take as given, take with modification (accept the problem, solve it differently), and push back with a reason. S6 suggests colour-coding a letter by how likely you are to make each change, and says to look for the root issue even when you reject the fix; S7 says editors are usually right about the problem and not always about the solution. Writing down the reason for every push back is a house setting. [S6] [S7]
- CR-TECH-06: Cut or fold in a scene where nothing substantive is happening, merge separate scenes that would be stronger as one, and merge minor characters who do the same job. S11 also suggests checking that each chapter advances at least two storylines. Asking whether the plot still makes sense without a scene is an editorial test (S11 does not state it); a scene that fails it may still earn its place for voice, atmosphere or setup. [S11]
- CR-TECH-07: Run targeted search passes (filter words, softeners, scaffolding such as "began to", repeated gestures) and treat each hit as a flag to review, not a word to delete. Use a voice audit on a sample: mark passages that sound like the book, that could be anyone, and that try too hard, then revise only the last two groups. [S9]
- CR-TECH-08: Read aloud, and gain distance first. Reading out loud makes each word heard and shows where the ear simplifies a sentence. A break of hours, days or weeks, or a fresh reader, helps a familiar text read as new. [S10]

## Pitfalls
- CR-PIT-01: Proofreading or line editing before the structure is settled. Fixing a plot hole in the proof stage can damage earlier rounds of editing. [S3] [S4]
- CR-PIT-02: Applying every note. Trying to apply every suggestion can leave a manuscript muddled, so weigh each note by its source and its fit to the book's purpose. [S6]
- CR-PIT-03: Ignoring a note because the fix is wrong. Something prompted the note (S6), and editors are usually right about the problem even when the fix is wrong (S7). If the writer needs a long explanation of why a change cannot work, that explanation may be missing from the manuscript (editorial judgment, not stated by the sources). [S6] [S7]
- CR-PIT-04: Flattening voice by rule-following. Cutting every adverb, long sentence or filter word, or accepting a rewrite that sounds like anyone, writes to a checklist instead of the story. Tight is not always short, and show-don't-tell is guidance, not an absolute. [S9]
- CR-PIT-05: Mistaking habits for voice. Repeated shrugs, nods and tightening chests are tics to fix, not personality to protect. [S9]
- CR-PIT-06: Treating every darling as sacred or every one as cuttable. Take a hard look at passages that exist because they are loved. Sometimes they are great and sometimes they belong in another book. A persistent urge to cut is usually worth heeding. [S11]
- CR-PIT-07: Fixing story structure and scene detail in the same pass until the work becomes overwhelming. Do the big-picture change first and refine scenes in the next draft. [S8] [S7]

## Checks
- CR-CHK-01: The revision plan is in order: structural issues are listed and resolved before any line or copyedit note is actioned on the same scenes. [S1] [S4]
  Check: Pass if the revision notes or run record show developmental or scene-level changes decided before line or copyedit edits to the same scenes. Fail if line or copyedit work was done on a scene that a structural note says to cut, merge or move.
- CR-CHK-02: Each note is triaged and has a recorded decision (take, take modified, push back with reason). The record itself is a house setting; S6 and S7 only say not to ignore notes blindly or obey them blindly. [S6] [S7]
  Check: Pass if every note in the review has a recorded decision and every push back has a reason. Fail if any note is silently unaddressed.
- CR-CHK-03: Every must-fix note names a concrete problem in the text, not only a taste preference. [S7]
  Check: Pass if each must-fix note points to a specific passage and names what is wrong (confusion, contradiction, stalled scene, broken continuity). Fail if a must-fix note says only that the reviewer did not like it or to consider changing it, with no problem stated.
- CR-CHK-04: Repeated notes are escalated. [S7]
  Check: Pass if any issue raised by more than one note or reviewer is marked as a priority. Fail if a repeated issue is classed as optional.
- CR-CHK-05: Each scene still does work. [S11]
  Check: Pass if each scene in the revised outline or manuscript changes something (a goal, relationship, knowledge or stakes shift), advances a storyline, or is kept for a stated reason (voice, atmosphere, setup, a deliberate pause). Fail if a scene where nothing substantive happens was kept with no stated reason, or if a scene was cut and a later scene still depends on it.
- CR-CHK-06: Revised passages keep the book's voice. [S9]
  Check: Pass if a sample of revised passages still matches the voice guidance in the style guide and the unrevised text (sentence rhythm, vocabulary, narrator attitude), or differs only where a recorded style-guide decision or the scene's purpose calls for it. Fail if revised passages read in a noticeably different or generic register than their neighbours with no such reason.
- CR-CHK-07: Line edits do not smuggle in structure changes, and copyedits do not rewrite. [S3] [S5]
  Check: Pass if the diff between drafts at the line or copyedit stage changes no plot events, character decisions or scene order, and a copyedit changes wording only to fix errors or inconsistency. Fail if a line or copyedit pass adds, removes or reorders events.
- CR-CHK-08: Continuity facts are consistent after revision. [S3] [S5]
  Check: Pass if names, dates, ages, places and objects named in a revised scene agree with canon and with other scenes. Fail if a revised scene contradicts a fact stated elsewhere, or if a cut scene left a later scene referring to something the reader never saw.
- CR-CHK-09: The revision does not loop. [S6] [S8]
  Check: Pass if each redraft round states the specific problem it fixes and that problem was not already fixed in an earlier round. Fail if a scene is rewritten again with no new note or stated goal, or if more than 3 redraft rounds on one scene occur without a new reason (house setting, tagged to S6 only for its warning that applying ever more suggestions muddles a manuscript).

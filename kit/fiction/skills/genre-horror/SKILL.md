# Horror

Layers on top of the audience profile. Where this skill and the audience profile disagree on length or content limits, the audience profile wins. Content limits for graphic violence always come from the audience profile, not from this skill.

## Reader promise
- HOR-PROM-01: Readers come for an emotion: painful, intense fear, dread or dismay. The Horror Writers Association page says horror is defined by that emotional response, not by ghosts or monsters. [S1]
- HOR-PROM-02: The reader enters the nightmare by choice. The HWA page quotes McCammon describing horror as a guide through that nightmare, one that can shock, illuminate and threaten. [S1]
- HOR-PROM-03: One field study of 110 haunted house visitors found enjoyment rose and then fell as fear rose (an inverted U), so treat a moderate fear level as a working assumption, not a law. [S5]
- HOR-PROM-04: Deliver the horror the premise promised. Agents name poor writing, weak structure and failing to deliver on the horror premise as common problems, and say that if the story is scary only when the monster is on the page, something is wrong. [S2]

## Structure
- HOR-STR-01: Begin the horror early without announcing it. Holding frightening material back makes readers wait too long. Make the first disturbing event easy to explain, such as a light left on or a dog that will not enter a room. [S3]
- HOR-STR-02: Make each new incident change the meaning of an earlier one, not just repeat it, and after each significant encounter remove something from the protagonist: an explanation, an assumption, support, safety or an escape. [S3]
- HOR-STR-03: Place one irreversible event before the climax, and build the climax from the patterns, rules and choices already set up, not from an unrelated monster. [S3]
- HOR-STR-04: Alternate calm with creeping discomfort. Slower rhythm, sensory focus and rising stakes, then a sudden shift, is often more effective than constant speed, and the payoff must match the build-up. [S4]
- HOR-STR-05: A range of 80,000 to 89,999 words is what Writer's Digest calls a safe one for adult commercial genres including horror, with under 70,000 too short and 110,000 or more too long. [S7]

## Conventions
- HOR-CONV-01: Give the threat consistent rules. Rules let readers anticipate, and a good rule gives the writer something to twist. Mystery is not confusion. [S3]
- HOR-CONV-02: Plant light-handed omens early, such as odd noises, missing objects or changed behaviour. Keep them ambiguous and do not over-explain. [S4]
- HOR-CONV-03: Make readers care about the people at risk. A scare lands harder on a character the reader is invested in, and stakes such as identity, memory and loved ones deepen dread. [S2][S4]
- HOR-CONV-04: Give the protagonist a reason to stay that comes from character, such as debt, a job, a missing child or need, and put costs on both staying and leaving. Do not use convenient obstacles like locked doors or dead cars. [S3]
- HOR-CONV-05: Use voice to create atmosphere. One agent says how the story is told is, in horror, almost more important than the story itself. [S2]
- HOR-CONV-06: Shift who knows more, reader or character, selectively. Constantly giving readers more information than the protagonist can drain mystery [S3], and if readers know exactly what is coming, dread can fade into expectation or boredom [S4].
- HOR-CONV-07: A dramatic ending is fine, but the climax must pay off what was built and something should remain changed. Research on whether a resolved threat makes horror more enjoyable is mixed, so do not assume a tidy ending is needed. [S3][S6]

## Pitfalls
- HOR-PIT-01: Do not use gore without characterization. Agents name too much gore with too little characterization as a common problem, and say blood is fine if it serves a purpose. [S2]
- HOR-PIT-02: Do not rely on stock premises. Agents list ghost houses, ancient evil in sleepy rural towns, the thing in the woods, and evil forces targeting a child as clichés. One tip is to keep a list of known clichés and rewrite any that appear. [S2]
- HOR-PIT-03: Do not repeat the same creepy incident with no new meaning, and do not move so slowly nothing seems to happen or reveal so fast that uncertainty vanishes. [S3]
- HOR-PIT-04: Do not leave gaps in the rules of the supernatural. One agent says gaps in worldbuilding shatter the illusion, and that if readers do not believe they will not feel fear. [S2]
- HOR-PIT-05: Do not make the protagonist foolish to explain why they stay, and do not delay all major consequences to the final act, which leaves dread weightless. [S3]
- HOR-PIT-06: Do not rely on creepy imagery alone, and do not overuse abrupt short sentences, which become predictable, or long descriptive stretches, which become sluggish. [S3]

## Reader psychology
Builds on the reader-engagement skill.
- HOR-ENG-01: Every on-page threat event is signalled by an earlier omen, warning or character fear, so dread comes before the event. [S4]
  Check: for each threat event, an earlier sentence or scene shows an omen, a warning or the character sensing danger.
- HOR-ENG-02: Each incident removes a harmless explanation the character or reader used before. [S3]
  Check: after each incident the text shows that an earlier explanation no longer works, or names it as failed.
- HOR-ENG-03: After each major scare scene, the text records a cost: the character loses an explanation, an ally, safety or an escape. [S3]
  Check: list what was taken from the protagonist in each major scare scene; the list is not empty.
- HOR-ENG-04: Two highest-intensity scenes are never adjacent without a lower-intensity scene between them (house setting). [S4]
  Check: label each scene's intensity high, medium or low; no two high scenes sit back to back.
- HOR-ENG-05: At least one scene in each act holds dread without the threat on the page, such as an omen or a broken routine (house setting). [S2][S4]
  Check: name a scene per act in which the threat is absent yet something is wrong.
- HOR-ENG-06: Before the first threat event, the text gives the reader a person to care about and something that person wants or stands to lose. [S2]
  Check: a named person and a want or a loss at risk appear before the first threat event.

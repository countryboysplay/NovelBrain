# Craft: POV, Distance and Dialogue

These are craft principles, not formulas. Working editors disagree at the edges, and published authors break most of them on purpose. The book's audience and genre skills and its style guide override this skill on content, length and voice. Where this skill says "default", the style guide may choose otherwise.

## Principles

- CP-PRIN-01: In close third person, give each scene one viewpoint character, ideally the one with the most at stake, and change viewpoint at a scene or chapter break. Changing viewpoint character is not the fault; the fault is switching mid-sentence, mid-paragraph or mid-scene by accident. [S3] [S4]
- CP-PRIN-02: Omniscient narration is a different mode from head-hopping. It works when it is established from the opening and the narrator's voice is clear. Do not drift into it. [S3]
- CP-PRIN-03: Narrative (psychic) distance is a dial, not a right answer. Wide distance suits context and sweep; close distance suits scenes where specific detail and feeling matter. Move it on purpose, and one level at a time rather than jumping from wide to very close. [S1]
- CP-PRIN-04: Choose one narrative tense and hold it. Past tense is the safe default that readers stop noticing; present tense is a legitimate choice with a cost in reader attention. A deliberate second timeline or frame in another tense needs a fixed rule used throughout. [S6]
- CP-PRIN-05: Only the viewpoint character's thoughts appear, and only thoughts that move the plot or raise emotional intensity earn space. Use one method of rendering thought, consistently, from first page to last. [S5]
- CP-PRIN-06: Every line of dialogue should earn its place by doing at least one job (develop character, advance plot, build tension), and the best lines do several at once. [S10] [S8]
- CP-PRIN-07: Characters say things for their own reasons, not for the reader's. Exposition in dialogue passes the test only when it makes sense for these characters to talk about this, at this moment. [S8]
- CP-PRIN-08: Showing and telling are both tools. Show what the reader should feel; tell what the reader only needs to know. If it can be told well, tell it. [S9]

## Techniques

- CP-TECH-01: Show another character's inner state from the viewpoint character's side: through body language, dialogue laden with subtext, a hedge such as "seemed", or a scene break at a moment of ambiguity, instead of entering that character's head. [S3] [S4]
- CP-TECH-02: If a viewpoint change inside a scene is truly wanted, signal it at once by naming the new viewpoint character in the opening words, and establish the pattern early so it does not look like a slip. [S3]
- CP-TECH-03: When you want the narration closer, remove filter verbs ("saw", "noticed", "heard") and presenting the perception directly. Anchor viewpoint with something only that character can feel, know or think, and match narration diction to that character, not to the author. [S4] [S9]
- CP-TECH-04: Choose how thoughts are marked and keep it: italics with a tag is widest, italics without a tag is closer, and no italics and no tag is the least intrusive and suits deep third or first person. Without italics, keep thoughts in the narration's tense and person. [S5]
- CP-TECH-05: Default to "said" and "asked", which readers barely register. Mix tags, action beats and untagged lines: a beat can show who is speaking, convey emotion or vary pace, and quick two-person exchanges can run untagged when the speaker is clear. A beat usually replaces a tag rather than sitting beside it. A more vivid verb is fine where it earns its place. [S10]
- CP-TECH-06: Put emotion in the line and the action, not in an adverb. If a line needs "said sharply", the line is probably not sharp enough. Letting what is not said carry weight builds subtext; a beat that contradicts the words is one way to do it (editorial judgment; S10 states the first point only). An adverb is fine when it adds something the line cannot. [S10]
- CP-TECH-07: Fix "as you know" exposition by dropping the dialogue and summarizing in narration, or by keeping the talk only where these characters would plausibly raise the subject, giving the facts in short pieces across a conversation instead of one monologue. A character who truly lacks the information asking about it is a fair option (editorial judgment; S8 does not list it as a fix). [S8]
- CP-TECH-08: Voice dialect through word choice, grammar, syntax and rhythm, with spelling left normal. If phonetic spelling is kept, use few markers and spell each word the same way every time. [S7]

## Pitfalls

- CP-PIT-01: Head-hopping: the reader gets a thought from a second character inside a scene anchored to the first, so the narration reports something the viewpoint character cannot know. It breaks intimacy and forces rereading. [S3] [S4]
- CP-PIT-02: Unmarked distance jumps: sudden, unexplained shifts between wide narration and very close interior, within a paragraph, without a purpose. [S1]
- CP-PIT-03: Tense slips: unmarked shifts between past and present in narration, or an unmarked present-tense thought dropped into past-tense narration. [S6] [S5]
- CP-PIT-04: Filter verbs and "seemed" used as a band-aid: a perception verb stands between reader and character, or hedges such as "seemed" or "appeared" are scattered through a scene instead of anchoring viewpoint properly. [S4] [S9]
- CP-PIT-05: Said-bookisms and adverb tags: showy verbs piled up, or tags that tell the emotion the line should carry, or a tag attached to every line so the rhythm goes monotonous. [S10]
- CP-PIT-06: "As you know, Bob": a character tells a listener what both already know, including the subtler version where the listener's reply ("I know, I know") reveals the speech was for the reader. [S8]
- CP-PIT-07: Phonetic dialect overload: heavy trick spellings make lines hard to read, or one character alone is rendered phonetically so they look lesser than the rest of the cast. [S7]
- CP-PIT-08: Telling a life-changing moment in one line, or showing a routine moment at length, or telling readers what they have just seen happen. [S9]

## Checks

- CP-CHK-01: One viewpoint character per scene is held; any viewpoint change falls at a scene or chapter break or is deliberately signalled. [S3] [S4]
  Check: Skip this check if the style guide declares omniscient narration or multi-viewpoint scenes. Otherwise list the viewpoint character of each scene. Fail if a scene gives a thought, sensation or private knowledge belonging to anyone other than that scene's viewpoint character, with no scene break or signalled shift before it.
- CP-CHK-02: The viewpoint character never reports what they cannot perceive or know. [S4]
  Check: In a limited-viewpoint book (not one the style guide declares omniscient, and allowing a retrospective narrator who openly knows more), fail if the narration states another character's inner thoughts, a fact the viewpoint character has not learned (for example a stranger's name, or the viewpoint character's own facial expression as seen from outside), or an event the viewpoint character was not present for, within that scene's text.
- CP-CHK-03: Narrative tense is consistent. [S6]
  Check: Fail if narration outside quoted dialogue, remembered speech and a declared frame or secondary timeline moves between past and present tense without a pattern stated in the style guide or visible in the text (for example, a fixed rule for flashbacks).
- CP-CHK-04: Distance changes are purposeful and graded. [S1]
  Check: Fail if two adjacent paragraphs move between wide summary narration and sensation-level interior with no transition and no apparent reason tied to the scene's emotion or action.
- CP-CHK-05: Interior thought is rendered one way throughout the book. [S5]
  Check: Fail if thoughts appear in more than one format (for example italic with a tag in one chapter and unmarked in another) without a style-guide reason, or if thoughts appear in quotation marks.
- CP-CHK-06: Dialogue lines each do work. [S10] [S8]
  Check: Pick any dialogue exchange. Fail if it can be removed without losing character information, plot information or tension, and it is not deliberate small talk used to dodge something.
- CP-CHK-07: Dialogue does not serve the reader at the characters' expense. [S8]
  Check: Fail if a character tells another something the second character plainly already knows (given earlier text or character state) and the scene gives no reason these two would say it now (S8's test: does it make sense for the characters to be talking about this). A deliberate reminder, quarrel over details or ritual retelling passes if the scene shows why.
- CP-CHK-08: Attribution stays out of the way and varies. [S10]
  Check: Fail if tags draw attention to themselves rather than the speech (showy verbs piled up, adverbs that merely restate the emotion the line already carries), or if a long exchange is monotonous because every line carries a tag or every line carries a beat. Distinctive verbs or adverbs that add something the line cannot, or a style-guide choice, pass.
- CP-CHK-09: Dialect is carried by word choice and syntax, applied evenly. [S7]
  Check: Fail if only one character's speech is respelled phonetically, if the same dialect word is spelled two ways, or if a line cannot be understood on first read.
- CP-CHK-10: Showing and telling are matched to the stakes of the moment. [S9]
  Check: Fail if a major turning point or emotional change is told in a single sentence of summary, or if a routine action receives sensory scene treatment that delays a scene's central event, unless the style guide or the scene makes the understatement or lingering deliberate and purposeful.

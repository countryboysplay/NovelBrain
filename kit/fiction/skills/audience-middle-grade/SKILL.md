# Middle grade (about ages 8-12)

## Length
- MG-LEN-01: Total length is 30,000-50,000 words as the working band. The outer range in use is 20,000-55,000, and fantasy or other heavy world-building may run to about 60,000. Do not go above 60,000 without a world-building reason stated in the plan (house setting for the 30,000-50,000 working band and the 60,000 cap; the 60,000 figure is the upper bound in S5). [S1][S2][S3][S5]
- MG-LEN-02: Younger middle grade (readers about 8-10) runs 20,000-35,000 words. Older middle grade (readers about 11-12) runs 40,000-55,000. Pick one band in the plan and stay in it (house setting). The split by reader age rests on S12 and S3 only, and S12 is a single editor's blog. [S3][S12]
- MG-LEN-03: Contemporary stories sit in the lower half of the band. Only fantasy, science fiction and historical stories that need world-building use the upper end. [S1][S2][S5]
- MG-LEN-04: Chapters run 700-2,000 words, and 2,000 is the working maximum. A chapter that covers one focused scene can be 700-1,500. A chapter over 3,000 words must be split or must contain a clear change of pace (house setting for the 700, 2,000 and 3,000 limits; S12 gives 1,000-2,000 and S13 gives 700-1,500 for one scene and 2,000-3,000 as a middle ground). [S12][S13]
- MG-LEN-05: Chapters that deliver a lot of information are shorter than the average chapter, and a dense chapter is never followed directly by another dense chapter. [S13]

## Language
- MG-LANG-01: Keep running text inside the Lexile text-measure bands used for grades 3-5, about 520L-1010L overall. Use roughly 520L-820L for the younger band and 740L-1010L for the older band (house setting: S9 publishes grade 3, 4 and 5 text bands of 520-820L, 740-940L and 830-1010L as descriptive only, and the younger and older split is derived from them). Lexile is driven by word frequency and sentence length. Check: run the sample through a Lexile or similar analyzer and report the measure. [S9]
- MG-LANG-02: Use simple vocabulary and sentence structure, but never talk down. An occasional sophisticated word is allowed when the sentence gives enough context to work out its meaning. [S2][S4][S12]
- MG-LANG-03: Hold one consistent narrating voice. The voice must not swing between sounding very young and sounding oddly precocious within a scene. [S4]
- MG-LANG-04: Dialogue sounds like present-day kids, but avoid current slang that will date the book. [S4][S12]
- MG-LANG-05: Use short sentences in action and longer sentences in quiet moments, because sentence length controls speed. [S12]
Declared gap: no source read gives a paragraph-length limit for middle grade, so none is enforced here.

## POV, tense and protagonist
- MG-POV-01: Third person is the most common choice, and first person is acceptable. Past tense is the default, and present tense is acceptable if used consistently. [S1][S2][S5]
- MG-POV-02: The protagonist is 10-12 for a book aimed at readers 8-12, and 13 only for older middle grade. Readers prefer to read up, so the hero is at the oldest age the story allows. A protagonist of 8 or younger belongs in chapter books. [S1][S2][S5][S12]
- MG-POV-03: Younger middle grade (about 8-10) uses one point of view. Older middle grade may add a second protagonist or point of view. [S12]
- MG-POV-04: The protagonist owns the central conflict and is the one who resolves it. Adults stay in side roles. [S4][S5]
- MG-POV-05: Realistic stories are set in the present day unless the plan states a compelling reason for another period. [S4]
- MG-POV-06: Characters mostly react to events and act, with little self-analysis. A passage of interior reflection is followed straight away by action or dialogue. [S1][S2]

## Content limits
These are defaults for the author and reviewer agents. Where no source states the limit, the rule says (house setting) and cites the closest source for its direction. No opened source gave a publisher or librarian rulebook for drugs, alcohol, animal cruelty or unresolved peril, so those limits are house settings.
- MG-CON-01: Romance stays at a crush or a first kiss at most, and it is secondary to the plot. No dating plot that reads like teen or adult romance. [S1][S2][S12]
- MG-CON-02: Violence and danger may be scary and dark but never graphic or persistent. Default (house setting for the detail level): an injury or fight is stated in a sentence or two with no blood-and-gore detail and no torture, and no scene dwells on pain. [S1][S2][S5]
- MG-CON-03: Sexual content: none beyond the crush or first kiss in MG-CON-01. No sexual language, no sexual threat. Default (house setting). [S1][S2]
- MG-CON-04: Profanity: none. Mild insults a child would actually use are the ceiling (house setting for the ceiling). [S1][S2][S12]
- MG-CON-05: Drugs and alcohol: none on the page by default. A background mention is allowed only with a plain consequence and no glamour, and the plan must record why (house setting). [S1][S2]
- MG-CON-06: Peril: the protagonist may face real danger, but the book resolves it by the end. No ending leaves a child in unresolved real peril, abuse or abandonment (house setting; direction supported by the hopeful-ending guidance). [S1][S11][S12]
- MG-CON-07: Death and grief may appear, including a parent or a friend, but the story is not graphic and the arc turns toward hope by the end. The main character is not left broken at the close. S11 is a single author blog and is weaker support. [S5][S11]
- MG-CON-08: Death of the lead or a sidekick on the page is not allowed by default. Deaths of secondary characters, or of a lead or sidekick away from the page and told afterwards, are allowed with the hope arc in MG-CON-07 (house setting; no source states this limit, S11 supports only that loss can appear when the book turns to hope, and S5 only that content be age-appropriate). [S5][S11]
- MG-CON-09: Animals may be hurt or die when the story needs it, but cruelty is never shown in detail and never played as spectacle (house setting; no source states this limit, S5 supports only that content be appropriate for the age group). [S5]
- MG-CON-10: Any scene, topic or image beyond the defaults in MG-CON-01 to MG-CON-09 is flagged for human review at the act checkpoint instead of being written through. The agent lists the scene, the rule it exceeds and the reason, and waits for the author to approve (house setting). [S1][S2]
- MG-CON-11: A heavy topic is allowed only if the book handles it with care and hope. Gratuitous sex, language or violence is never allowed. Write as if parents, teachers and librarians are reading, because they act as gatekeepers. [S1][S2]

## Pacing and themes
- MG-PACE-01: Themes come from the child's own world: friendship, family, fitting in, self-discovery and identity. [S1][S2][S5]
- MG-PACE-02: Give the protagonist problems to solve and decisions to make (S4, S12; S12 does not use the word independence), and let those decisions have consequences (house setting). [S4][S12]
- MG-PACE-03: The ending gives closure and hope. All loose ends are resolved and the ending is not vague. [S1][S11][S12]
- MG-PACE-04: Open with a concrete problem and fast action. One agent quoted in S10 reports that editors show little interest in quiet stories and ask for high stakes and a quick pace (single-agent support). [S10]
- MG-PACE-05: Alternate information-dense passages with scenes of dialogue or movement, so no long run of explanation sits between two action scenes. [S13]
- MG-PACE-06: A moral or lesson is something the protagonist discovers, shown through events, and it is never delivered as a speech to the reader. [S4][S12]

## Reader psychology
Builds on the reader-engagement skill.
- MG-ENG-01: Every chapter ends on a hook: a new complication, a revelation, a new character, a decision about to be made, or a strong emotion. [S5][S6]
  Check: the last 150 words (house setting) of the chapter introduce or leave open one specific new problem, fact, character or choice.
- MG-ENG-02: After a surprise or cliffhanger, the chapter ends on the protagonist's reaction, not on the bare event. [S6]
  Check: the final paragraph contains the protagonist's thought, feeling or action in response to the last event.
- MG-ENG-03: No chapter, except the final one, ends on the character noting that things are calm or settled. [S6]
  Check: search the last paragraph of each chapter for a reflection that the situation is calm, safe or resolved with no new tension. Any hit fails.
- MG-ENG-04: Vary chapter endings so the hook does not become a repeated trick. No more than two chapters in a row end with the same device (cliffhanger line, question or reveal). S6 warns that repeated cliffhangers lose force but gives no number, so the limit of two is a (house setting). [S6]
  Check: label each chapter ending by device and fail if three consecutive labels match.
- MG-ENG-05: Every chapter holds at least one deliberate humour beat, even in a dark or tense chapter. Seven in ten children say they look for books that make them laugh, and humour is the top thing 9-11 year olds say they look for. The one-beat-per-chapter minimum is a (house setting); S7 gives only survey percentages. [S7][S12]
  Check: quote two lines per chapter that a reader could be expected to laugh or smile at, each from a different paragraph, such as a joke, a comic reaction or an absurd comparison. A chapter with fewer than two quoted lines is a low-humour chapter, and the check fails if any chapter has none.
- MG-ENG-06: The protagonist shows one trait readers admire (smart, strong or brave) and one flaw through action in the first chapter, so readers can identify with the character (house setting for the first-chapter timing). [S5][S7]
  Check: quote two lines from chapter 1, one where the protagonist does something smart, strong or brave (a visible act with a result) and one where the protagonist makes a mistake or shows a weakness. Both must be actions or dialogue, not narrator statements.
- MG-ENG-07: No adult makes the decision or performs the action that resolves the central conflict. Adults may give information or a resource, but never arrive late to fix the problem. [S4]
  Check: name who performs the decisive action in the climax. It must be the protagonist, with peers at most. Any adult who solves the problem fails.
- MG-ENG-08: Each chapter turns on something the protagonist chooses or does, not on luck or on what an adult does (house setting at chapter level; S5 supports a proactive protagonist who drives the plot, S4 keeps adults out of the resolution). [S4][S5]
  Check: for each chapter, quote the protagonist choice or action that causes the chapter's main change. A chapter with none fails.
- MG-ENG-09: The story's central problem and what the protagonist wants are visible by the end of chapter 1. [S10]
  Check: quote the sentence that states or shows the problem and the sentence that shows what the protagonist wants. Both must appear by the end of chapter 1.

## Put-down risks
- MG-RISK-01: Preaching makes young readers lose interest quickly. No adult or narrator pause to state a big moral point. [S4]
- MG-RISK-02: Talking down to readers is a put-down risk. Assume the reader can follow nuance, difficult subjects and a plot with layers. [S4]
- MG-RISK-03: A voice that drifts between very young and overly precocious is the most frequently reported flaw in submissions. [S4]
- MG-RISK-04: Adults who deliver life lessons, make plot-resolving choices, or take the lead while the child watches are a put-down risk. [S4]
- MG-RISK-05: A slow or quiet opening risks losing the reader. A chapter break is a natural stopping point, so do not leave a stretch of calm exposition near it. [S6][S10]
- MG-RISK-06: Length and stamina matter. Children's stamina for long books has dropped, and longer text multiplies the effect of hard language. Keeping the book at the low end of its band is an inference from S8 and S10, not a stated rule (house setting). [S8][S10]
- MG-RISK-07: Books that stop being funny and entertaining and books that get much harder are reasons children give for reading less for fun as they age. [S7]
- MG-RISK-08: Setting a realistic story in the author's own childhood makes it feel dated to current readers. [S4]

## Re-engagement
- MG-RE-01: The first lines of every chapter re-orient a reader who put the book down. Within the first three sentences, state who is present, where they are and what the immediate goal or problem is. The count of three is a (house setting). S6 says the chapter break is where readers stop, and the re-orientation rule is derived from that. [S6]
  Check: the first three sentences of each chapter name the character, the place and the current goal or problem.
- MG-RE-02: Do not hold the protagonist's emotional reaction back to the start of the next chapter. Put the reaction before the break. [S6]
  Check: the protagonist's reaction to the last big event appears before the chapter break, not in the next chapter's opening lines.
- MG-RE-03: Each chapter completes a small unit of change, so a reader who stops there still feels progress. Something is gained, lost, learned or decided. This is a (house setting) applied at chapter level. S10 makes its confidence point only about finishing whole shorter books. [S10]
  Check: name the one thing that is different at the end of each chapter compared with its start.
- MG-RE-04: Break up any chapter that runs long, with a cliffhanger and a restart or another change of pace, because long stretches slow the reader and tire stamina. [S8][S13]
  Check: any chapter over 2,000 words has a scene break or pattern interrupt before the 2,000-word mark.

# Reader engagement (all audiences)

## Mechanisms
- RE-MECH-01: Curiosity comes from a perceived gap in what the reader knows, so give each scene a specific open question the reader can see. [S1]
- RE-MECH-02: Suspense is felt before an event, not after it, so show the threatening or pending event ahead of its outcome. [S2]
- RE-MECH-03: The strength of tension depends on how much the possible outcome matters, so establish what the character wants and what is lost before the pending event. [S2]
- RE-MECH-04: Uncertainty can concern what will happen, how, when or whether, so vary which of these a scene leaves open. [S2]
- RE-MECH-05: Identification with a character is a distinct audience response to characters, so plan for it as its own aim. [S3]
- RE-MECH-06: Making the protagonist likable is one way writers foster identification and empathy in the reader. [S2]
- RE-MECH-07: Transportation is absorption into a story made of imagery, emotion and focused attention, so give key scenes concrete sensory detail and a felt emotional reaction. [S4]
- RE-MECH-08: Tension resolves when the open question is answered, and each resolution can start the next episode of tension, so answer what you open. [S2]
- RE-MECH-09: Unresolved suspense is uncomfortable, and resolving it makes readers more likely to move on to the next installment, so a cliffhanger can pull a reader forward. [S5]
- RE-MECH-10: In one study, enjoyment of cliffhanger endings fell between enjoyment of hero-win and villain-win endings, so a cliffhanger neither guarantees nor ruins enjoyment; use it as one ending type among several. [S5]
- RE-MECH-11: Calmer scenes let readers regroup, but they still need conflict, so vary intensity without letting a quiet scene go static. [S6]
- RE-MECH-12: Cliffhangers used too often make for bad transitions between chapters and scenes, so use them selectively. [S6]

## Reader psychology
Applies to every audience. Audience skills add their own thresholds.
- RE-ENG-01: Every scene gives the reader at least one question they can state in a sentence. [S1]
  Check: after reading the scene, you can write one specific unanswered question about it from the text alone.
- RE-ENG-02: The reader learns what the POV character wants and what is at risk before a peak event happens. [S2]
  Check: a sentence before the peak event states the character's goal or what could be lost.
- RE-ENG-03: A threatening event is named before it occurs, not only after (house setting: the signal must come in an earlier sentence than the event). [S2]
  Check: the text first signals the coming event earlier than the line where it happens.
- RE-ENG-04: Every question a chapter opens is either answered later or recorded as carried forward with the chapter where it will be answered. [S2]
  Check: list each question the chapter raises; pass only if every one has a named later passage that answers it or an entry in the open-questions record (maintained by the showrunner) naming its planned answer chapter.
- RE-ENG-05: The last scene of each chapter leaves a question open, a decision pending, or a deliberate resolution, and the last three chapters do not all end the same way (house setting: three-chapter window). [S6]
  Check: label the last three chapter endings as open, pending or resolved; they are not all one label.
- RE-ENG-06: A low-intensity scene still contains an unresolved conflict, question or threat. [S6]
  Check: point to one line in each quiet scene that states a conflict, question or threat still unresolved.
- RE-ENG-07: Key events are shown through what the POV character perceives, thinks or feels (house setting, a way to support identification). [S3]
  Check: every key event in the scene is paired with a perception or reaction by the POV character, and none is something the POV character could not know.
- RE-ENG-08: The peak scene of each chapter has concrete sensory detail and a stated emotional reaction (house setting: one of each at minimum). [S4]
  Check: the peak scene contains at least one sensory detail and one named or shown emotional reaction from the POV character.
- RE-ENG-09: Consecutive scenes do not all sit at the same intensity (house setting: two-level high or low labelling). [S2][S6]
  Check: label each scene in the chapter high or low intensity; both labels appear.
- RE-ENG-10: The protagonist gives the reader a reason to care early in the book (house setting: within the first chapter). [S2]
  Check: within the first chapter the protagonist shows a want, a vulnerability or a kindness that a reader can name.

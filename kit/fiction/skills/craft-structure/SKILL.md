# Craft: Story Structure

These are craft principles, not formulas. Working editors and authors disagree on names, counts and percentages, so this skill describes what structural beats do and offers several frameworks as options. The book's audience and genre skills and its style guide override this skill on content, length and voice. The outline picks exactly one framework per book (see CS-PRIN-01); the others are vocabulary for diagnosis, not extra requirements.

## Principles
- CS-PRIN-01: Frameworks are options, and the outline picks one per book and records which. Three-act (about 25 percent, 50 percent, 25 percent for acts one to three in most three-act stories) [S11], the Save the Cat beat sheet with 15 beats at stated percentages [S1], Vogler's twelve-stage hero's journey [S12], the seven-point structure [S7] and the Story Grid five commandments [S2] overlap heavily but are not identical, and one source notes that most novels do not follow the classic models [S4]. Percentages describe common shapes, not targets. Mixing labels from several frameworks in one outline invites contradictions, so name one and map the others to it only when diagnosing.
- CS-PRIN-02: Structure is a chain of cause and effect, not a list of events. Each major event should cause the next, and trials should escalate and link causally rather than being episodic. [S4] [S11]
- CS-PRIN-03: Many frameworks can be mapped onto one functional spine, which Story Grid states as five parts: an inciting disturbance that unbalances the protagonist, complications that defeat each strategy, a forced choice (the crisis), an active answer to it (the climax), and consequences of that answer (the resolution). Use it as a diagnostic, not a requirement for every book. [S2]
- CS-PRIN-04: The first act ends when the protagonist commits and cannot easily go back (first plot point, "Break into Two", "Crossing the First Threshold"). [S11] [S1] [S12]
- CS-PRIN-05: The midpoint usually changes the direction of the story. Sources describe it as a false victory or false defeat that raises stakes [S1], as a reversal where the story seems over but is not [S4], or as the shift from reaction to action [S7]. Pick the definition that fits the chosen framework; a quiet or internal midpoint counts if what the protagonist is doing or what is at stake visibly changes.
- CS-PRIN-06: In the three-act and Save the Cat models the low point comes late in act two: the protagonist seems sure to fail, or the feared thing happens, and the act ends on a turning point into act three. Seven-point structure reaches the same place by making the second pinch as dire as possible. It may be a loss, a death or a quiet collapse of hope, depending on genre. [S11] [S1] [S7]
- CS-PRIN-07: The crisis is a real choice between incompatible options with meaningful stakes: either the best of two bad choices or a pick between two goods. The climax is the active answer, and it shows who the character is under pressure. [S2]
- CS-PRIN-08: A subplot needs its own line of change and must affect the main plot, not merely share its theme. [S10]
- CS-PRIN-09: Setup creates an obligation. A detail planted as significant (object, skill, rule, trait, fact) needs a payoff, while details that serve character, setting or tone need none. [S9]
- CS-PRIN-10: The ending delivers on the promises made at the start and ties off loose ends, with enough room after the climax for the reader to land. [S8] [S11]

## Techniques
- CS-TECH-01: Plan backward. Decide the resolution first, then choose a hook whose state is far from or opposite to it, then fill in the plot turns, pinches and midpoint. [S7]
- CS-TECH-02: Write each major middle attempt as a try-fail cycle: the character makes the smartest move available given what they know, and the result is "yes, but" (success with a new complication) or "no, and" (failure that makes things worse). [S6] [S5]
- CS-TECH-03: Make every cycle escalate. Each round should raise stakes, flip the situation or increase danger, and the character should learn something, so the next attempt differs from the last. [S5] [S6]
- CS-TECH-04: Mirror the ending into the middle. Plan the climax first, then make middle failures prepare it, for example a failure to trust something that is later trusted at the climax. [S6]
- CS-TECH-05: Scale cycles to importance. Do not force a fixed count of failures; the number should reflect how much the problem matters to the protagonist, and complications should tie back to the central story instead of spawning new plots. [S6]
- CS-TECH-06: Plant with a job. Give a planted element a present-tense function in its first scene, and hide it among other plausible details so it is noticeable without announcing how it will matter. [S9]
- CS-TECH-07: Braid subplots through shared resources (time, information, access, trust, money, reputation) and cross them at main-plot turning points, so events in one thread create new conditions in the other. [S10]
- CS-TECH-08: Summarize each subplot in four beats (starting condition, first complication, major shift, outcome) before weaving it. If every beat reads "they keep having problems", it needs more progression. The source offers this as a planning step, not a required count. [S10]
- CS-TECH-09: Use the same unit of shape at every scale. The five commandments apply to a beat, scene, sequence, act, subplot and the whole story, so a chapter or sequence can be shaped as a small story that turns a value. [S3] [S2]
- CS-TECH-10: Let the subplot supply something the climax needs (a resource, a way out, an emotional decision) so the climax feels set up rather than contrived. [S10] [S4]

## Pitfalls
- CS-PIT-01: Episodic middles. Trials that could be reordered or removed without consequence read as a list rather than escalation. [S4]
- CS-PIT-02: A protagonist who never fails. If they are not failing they are probably not making real attempts, and easy wins make the eventual victory feel unearned. [S5] [S6]
- CS-PIT-03: Suspicion-confirmation middles. Building the middle mainly from a character suspecting something and having it quickly confirmed with little cost is a weak substitute for active failure cycles. [S5]
- CS-PIT-04: Treating a framework as a template. Strict compliance can produce forced cycles and mechanical beats, and the sources caution that bestselling books do not follow clean templates. [S4] [S6]
- CS-PIT-05: Underlining the plant. Emphasizing a setup so heavily that readers can see the payoff coming, or giving too little setup so the payoff feels convenient. [S9]
- CS-PIT-06: Broken promises. A planted significant detail or a red herring that never comes full circle leaves readers dissatisfied. [S9]
- CS-PIT-07: Parallel subplots that only alternate chapters. Sharing a character or a theme is not enough; if removing the thread changes nothing downstream, it is running in parallel. [S10]
- CS-PIT-08: A rushed ending. Pacing that works until the finale and then hurries the falling action makes readers feel the writer lost interest. [S8]
- CS-PIT-09: Scenes that do not turn. Story Grid defines a scene by a value shift, so a scene with no turning point and no shift is incomplete by that definition and is a candidate for cutting, merging or reshaping. The shift can be small or internal. [S3]

## Checks
- CS-CHK-01: The outline names one structural framework for the book and places the inciting incident, first-act break, midpoint, low point, climax and resolution in the story. [S1] [S2] [S11]
  Check: Pass if the outline states one framework by name and identifies a scene or event for that framework's equivalent of each of: inciting incident, first-act commitment, midpoint, low point, climax, resolution (house setting: map a framework without a named midpoint or low point to its nearest turn, for example Story Grid's progressive complication). Fail if any equivalent is missing without a stated reason, or if two frameworks are used as competing requirements.
- CS-CHK-02: The inciting incident visibly upsets the protagonist's situation and leads them to set a goal. [S2]
  Check: Pass if the text of the inciting scene shows a change to the protagonist's circumstances and a later scene shows them pursuing a stated goal as a result. Fail if the protagonist's situation and goal are unchanged after it.
- CS-CHK-03: The midpoint changes the direction or stakes. [S1] [S4] [S7]
  Check: Pass if, after the midpoint event, the protagonist's strategy, knowledge or stakes differ from before it in a way the text states. Fail if the story continues with the same plan and the same stakes.
- CS-CHK-04: Middle attempts escalate rather than repeat. [S5] [S6]
  Check: Pass if each major attempt in the middle ends in a failure or a costly success that worsens or complicates the protagonist's position, and each attempt differs in method, cost or danger from the previous one. Fail if two consecutive attempts are the same kind of event with the same result, unless the outline marks a deliberately quiet or circular middle and states what changes instead (relationship, knowledge, belief).
- CS-CHK-05: The crisis is a real dilemma and the climax answers it with an action. [S2]
  Check: Pass if the text before the climax states two incompatible options with costs, and the climax scene shows the protagonist acting on one of them. Fail if no choice is posed or the outcome happens by coincidence or outside action.
- CS-CHK-06: Every significant planted detail pays off and every payoff was planted. [S9]
  Check: Pass if each object, skill, rule or fact that decides the climax or a major turn appears in an earlier scene, and each detail the text flags as important is used later or is a deliberate red herring that is resolved. Fail if a deciding element first appears at the moment it is needed, or a flagged detail is never used.
- CS-CHK-07: Each subplot changes the main plot and has its own progression. [S10]
  Check: Pass if each subplot can be summarized in four beats (start, complication, shift, outcome; house setting: the count follows the source's planning step and is not a limit) and at least one subplot event changes a main-plot choice, resource or piece of information. Fail if a subplot's scenes could be deleted with no later scene changing.
- CS-CHK-08: Subplots are resolved or deliberately left open by the end. [S8] [S11]
  Check: Pass if each subplot ends in a scene that states its outcome by the close of the book, or the outline marks it as intentionally open with a reason (for example a series thread). Fail if a subplot simply stops appearing.
- CS-CHK-09: The ending resolves the question the story raised and gives the falling action room. [S8] [S2]
  Check: Pass if the outline or text states the central question or goal from the opening and the final scenes answer it, with a closing passage after the climax (a scene or part of one) showing consequences. Fail if the question is never answered or the book ends on the climax with no consequences shown, unless the outline gives a reason for an abrupt ending.
- CS-CHK-10: Each scene or chapter turns something. [S3]
  Check: Pass if, for a sampled chapter, one sentence can state what is different at its end (a value, goal, piece of knowledge, relationship or inner stance) compared to its start; the difference may be small or internal. Fail if no such difference can be stated and the outline gives no reason (for example a deliberate breather or atmosphere chapter).

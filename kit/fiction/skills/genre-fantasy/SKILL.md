# Fantasy

Layers on top of the audience profile. Where this skill and the audience profile disagree on length or content limits, the audience profile wins.

## Reader promise
- FAN-PROM-01: Readers come for wonder from exploring a created world, and for stakes that reach beyond one person's life. Authors of epic fantasy name wonder, exploration and world-changing stakes as core to the genre. [S5]
- FAN-PROM-02: Commercial fantasy is bought for a captivating voice, a quick pace and a primarily character-driven narrative, plus fresh takes on worldbuilding or magic systems. [S8]
- FAN-PROM-03: Wonder never replaces character. Characters must not be swamped by wonder, and every worldbuilding decision should have consequences for them. [S5]
- FAN-PROM-04: Readers enjoy assembling the world from clues, though some find that hard work, so signal clearly what matters and trust the reader with the rest. [S6]

## Structure
- FAN-STR-01: Open inside a moving scene with a character the reader can follow. Agents name an opening battle before the reader knows anyone, a pastoral scene of herb-gathering, and a prologue of backstory as openings they dislike. [S4]
- FAN-STR-02: Make failure cost other people, not only the protagonist. Several epic fantasy authors say epic stakes must affect more than the lead and, in the strongest view, change the world. One author (Rothfuss) dissents from equating epic with clashing armies. [S5]
- FAN-STR-03: Writer's Digest calls 100,000 to 115,000 words an excellent range for fantasy and science fiction, with 90,000 to 100,000 and 115,000 to 124,000 probably all right. Long debuts are risky, so do not exceed 124,000 words without a recorded reason (house setting). [S7]
- FAN-STR-04: Introduce culture, history, art and magic only when plot and character need them in the scene at hand. [S2]
- FAN-STR-05: Hold back worldbuilding that the current story does not use. One cited example withholds how a court works until a later book, where it serves character development. [S2]

## Conventions
- FAN-CONV-01: Establish what the magic can and cannot do, and what it costs, before it resolves a conflict. The author's ability to solve conflict with magic is directly proportional to how well the reader understands it. [S1]
- FAN-CONV-02: Treat magic as hard or soft on purpose. Soft magic suits wonder and ambiance and works best creating problems that people solve themselves. Hard magic can resolve conflict because readers understand what it can do. [S1]
- FAN-CONV-03: Before drafting, answer the basics in notes: whether natural laws differ from real life, where magic comes from, whether it can run out, and what its limits and long-term costs are. The SFWA worldbuilding checklist poses these as questions. [S3]
- FAN-CONV-04: Build characters from the same history and culture that shaped the world, so the story could only happen to that character in that world. [S5]
- FAN-CONV-05: Names, place names and product names carry information, so use them as signals of what to take for granted and what matters. [S6]

## Pitfalls
- FAN-PIT-01: Do not open with a prologue that lectures history. Most agents dislike prologues, and one calls them a lazy way to hand over backstory in chunks. [S4]
- FAN-PIT-02: Do not stop the story for a long account of the world. A big info dump backfires with readers, and too much telling in chapter one reads as a road map instead of a scene. [S2][S4]
- FAN-PIT-03: Do not invent a new power or a new rule at the moment of crisis. A law added for one situation makes the magic seem flimsy and convenient. [S1]
- FAN-PIT-04: Do not let modern attitudes slip into characters who live in the setting. One epic fantasy author calls characters carrying the wrong sensibilities modern intrusions. [S5]
- FAN-PIT-05: Do not list physical description. One agent calls laundry-list description pointless and says to work it into the story. [S4]
- FAN-PIT-06: Balance detail. Too much bores the reader and too little leaves the story shallow, and an undefined limit, such as a wizard's foresight, can create a plot hole. [S2]

## Reader psychology
Builds on the reader-engagement skill.
- FAN-ENG-01: Every new world term or magic rule that appears in a scene is tied to something a character wants, risks or decides in that same scene. [S2][S5]
  Check: for each new invented term in the scene, the same scene shows a character acting on it, losing something to it or choosing because of it.
- FAN-ENG-02: No stretch of pure world explanation runs past 150 words without a character action, thought or line of dialogue (house setting). [S2]
  Check: count words between character actions or dialogue in any explanatory passage; none exceeds 150.
- FAN-ENG-03: Before magic resolves a conflict, an earlier scene has already shown that ability and stated its limit or cost. [S1]
  Check: search earlier text for the first showing of the ability used at the resolution; it exists and a limit or cost is stated before the resolution scene.
- FAN-ENG-04: The first scene puts a named or clearly identified character in action with a want before any backstory block. The first 500 words (house setting) hold no history lecture. [S4]
  Check: within the first 500 words the character does something and wants something; no paragraph there narrates history with no character in it.
- FAN-ENG-05: Each unfamiliar term can be understood from the sentences around it, without a glossary, so the reader assembles meaning from clues. [S6]
  Check: for each invented term, the paragraph holding its first use has a context clue to its meaning (house setting: same paragraph or the next).
- FAN-ENG-06: Before the climax the text names who besides the protagonist is harmed if they fail. [S5]
  Check: a sentence before the climax states the people, place or thing beyond the lead that is lost on failure.

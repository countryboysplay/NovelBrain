# Thriller

Layers on top of the audience profile. Where this skill and the audience profile disagree on length or content limits, the audience profile wins. A thriller runs on danger that looks forward: will the hero stop what is coming, and at what price.

## Reader promise
- THR-PROM-01: A thriller centres on growing danger, with a protagonist under serious, often physical threat and short on time. Its defining trait is rising stakes, not violence or speed. [S1]
- THR-PROM-02: A mystery looks back to work out what happened. A thriller looks forward at what is about to happen, so keep the danger structural. If it fades midway the book is no longer a thriller. [S1]
- THR-PROM-03: Readers come for stress, not comfort. They want dread and shocks, then a resolution that leaves them wrecked but satisfied. [S4]
- THR-PROM-04: The opening is a promise of the ride ahead, such as a murder, a discovered body or a chase, and the book closes by fulfilling that promise. [S3]

## Structure
- THR-STR-01: The usual arc is inciting event, complications, a midpoint shift in understanding, then a peak-danger climax. Chapter one should shatter normal life. [S1][S4]
- THR-STR-02: Every major beat leaves the protagonist worse off. Middles fail most often because the character stays busy while the situation does not get harder. [S1]
- THR-STR-03: To save a sagging middle, introduce a new deadline, twist the knife after each win, flip allies, add reframing revelations and force choices with no good option. [S4]
- THR-STR-04: Build a power arc. The villain starts much stronger than the protagonist, and at the climax the positions flip. [S3][S2]
- THR-STR-05: Let the protagonist score occasional wins, ideally ones that yield information that brings stronger opposition and makes things worse. [S2]
- THR-STR-06: Resolve the central threat directly, without coincidence or a rescue from nowhere, and keep the aftermath brief because a long epilogue drains the climax. [S1]
- THR-STR-07: The typical length range is 70,000 to 100,000 words, with 80,000 to 90,000 typical. Domestic and psychological thrillers skew shorter, and spy, legal and ensemble thrillers skew longer. [S1]

## Conventions
- THR-CONV-01: Use a ticking clock. The protagonist and the reader both know the deadline, and the writer honours it, so the event happens or is prevented exactly when predicted. [S2]
- THR-CONV-02: A clock can be literal (bombs, deadlines) or psychological (fading hope, fraying sanity, a villain about to strike again), and each failure can move the hero closer to disaster. [S4]
- THR-CONV-03: Stakes are concrete and personal, and the hero has a reason they cannot simply walk away. If a sensible person would flee, give the protagonist a compelling reason to stay. [S1][S2]
- THR-CONV-04: The antagonist is the engine of escalation. Make them competent, well-resourced and motivated, present throughout and not only at the climax, and able to believe they are right. [S1][S4][S3]
- THR-CONV-05: Use dramatic irony. Letting the reader know of a danger the protagonist does not, such as a bomb under a table, builds suspense better than a surprise. [S2][S4]
- THR-CONV-06: The protagonist's own choices, skill or hard-won knowledge decide the outcome, and they cannot be a passenger at their own climax. Flaws and something real to lose give them stakes. [S1][S4]
- THR-CONV-07: Pick the subgenre early, since each carries expectations about pace, voice and detail: psychological, legal, spy, domestic, techno or crime and action. [S1]
- THR-CONV-08: First person or close third keeps the tension in what the character does not yet know. Add other viewpoints, including the villain's, only when they add tension the original could not. [S1]

## Pitfalls
- THR-PIT-01: A sagging middle where scenes do not make anything harder. Ask what is worse by the end of each scene and rebuild or cut scenes where nothing is. [S1]
- THR-PIT-02: A protagonist who escapes trouble easily leaves nothing thrilling, and so do perfect heroes, purely evil villains and unmotivated villains. [S3]
- THR-PIT-03: Over-explanation and backstory stall momentum, including research dumps on procedure or technology, exposition inside tense scenes and backstory in the middle of a chase. [S1][S2][S4]
- THR-PIT-04: Cliffhangers on every chapter annoy readers and feel cheap, so use them sparingly. [S3][S4]
- THR-PIT-05: Convenient coincidences, rescues from nowhere and telegraphed twists weaken the book. A twist must be fair in hindsight, with its clues planted in the first half. [S4][S1]
- THR-PIT-06: Nonstop action becomes noise. Alternate intense scenes with quieter breathing room. [S1]

## Reader psychology
Builds on the reader-engagement skill.
- THR-ENG-01: Chapter one puts the protagonist in trouble or poses a sharp question within its first 1,000 words (house setting). [S1][S4]
  Check: within the first 1,000 words you can point to a threat, a loss or a question the POV character cannot ignore.
- THR-ENG-02: State the clock in one sentence in the story notes, and the text reminds the reader of it at least once in each quarter of the manuscript (house setting). [S4][S2]
  Check: quote the one-sentence clock, then find one on-page reminder of it in each quarter.
- THR-ENG-03: Each scene ends with the protagonist worse off, or with a win that costs something or brings stronger opposition. [S1][S2]
  Check: write one line saying what got worse or what the win cost; if you cannot, the scene fails.
- THR-ENG-04: At least once per act (house setting) the reader learns of a danger before the protagonist does, so the reader waits with dread. [S2][S4]
  Check: find a scene in each act where the text shows the threat before the character knows of it.
- THR-ENG-05: After each peak-danger scene comes a quieter scene that reveals character, plants information or raises a question. No scene leaves things unchanged. [S1]
  Check: for each quiet scene, name what it reveals, plants or asks.
- THR-ENG-06: Chapters end at peak tension, but not every chapter ends on a cliffhanger. [S3][S4]
  Check: count chapters that end on an unresolved moment; the check fails if all of them do.

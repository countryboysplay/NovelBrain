# Historical fiction

Layers on top of the audience profile. Where this skill and the audience profile disagree on length or content limits, the audience profile wins.

## Reader promise
- HIS-PROM-01: Readers expect the author to make a best attempt at historical accuracy and to tell a good story. [S1]
- HIS-PROM-02: Readers of the genre love history and are frequently deeply knowledgeable, so do not assume the history does not matter. Obvious mistakes can cast doubt on the author's overall research. [S5][S4][S1]
- HIS-PROM-03: Readers want the past to feel alive and to come away with a deeper sense of history. [S4]
- HIS-PROM-04: Accuracy is not the only thing publishers look for. In the author's survey, editors said setting is not nearly as important as plot, character development and overall theme (she is skeptical for ancient and medieval settings), and literary historical fiction stresses themes that pertain back to the present. [S1]

## Structure
- HIS-STR-01: Let plot and character carry the book, with the setting as the stage. Literary historical fiction centres its tale on plot and on characters who speak to readers today, not on the setting. [S1]
- HIS-STR-02: Weave history into the story through events. One example is an aristocrat who runs down a child and tosses the father a coin, which shows the forces behind the French Revolution without summarizing them. [S3]
- HIS-STR-03: Be selective. One editor suggests discarding about 95 percent of the research while keeping the notes. [S3]
- HIS-STR-04: Do not re-order or re-locate major events, or put famous figures where they could not have been, unless writing alternate history or fantasy. [S5]
- HIS-STR-05: Fit period details around the plot, not the other way around. [S3]

## Conventions
- HIS-CONV-01: Set a consistent rule for when to depart from history and keep to it. Changing verifiable facts is different from filling gaps in the record, and any change must be believable. [S3]
- HIS-CONV-02: Write dialogue in neutral language with a few archaic words readers still recognize. Avoid both modern colloquialisms and true period language, which readers would find unreadable. [S3][S4]
- HIS-CONV-03: Choose words that fit the period, setting and characters. A word can be fine in narration and wrong in a character's mouth, as with "sadist" before the late 19th century. [S2]
- HIS-CONV-04: Make names suit the time and place. [S4]
- HIS-CONV-05: Get tools, food, jobs, houses, transport and pastimes right for the period. [S4]
- HIS-CONV-06: Include an author's note that summarizes major departures from the record, separates real from invented characters and may list sources. It is a simple act of transparency. [S5]
- HIS-CONV-07: When a character defies convention, show the obstacles, conflict and ostracism, so nonconformity reads as exceptional. [S3]
- HIS-CONV-08: Check when a word or expression came into use before putting it in speech, using period texts and word-dating references. Distant periods allow more freedom because fewer sources can contradict the writer. [S2]

## Pitfalls
- HIS-PIT-01: Do not dump research. Including everything produces a dry tome of a history book, and critics describe the weak historical novel as a barely fictionalized textbook. [S3][S1]
- HIS-PIT-02: Do not write modern people in period costume. Critics describe the weak historical novel as a costume drama, and an agent calls pasting 21st century sensibilities on historical characters jarring, with the nickname "Plantagenets in Pasadena". [S1][S4]
- HIS-PIT-03: Do not let anachronisms slip into speech or detail. One wrong word can prick the bubble of belief [S2], and obvious errors can cast doubt on the author's overall research [S1].
- HIS-PIT-04: Do not write caricatures of real people, and be cautious about vilifying them. Add nuance or invent the villain instead. [S5]
- HIS-PIT-05: Do not write full period pastiche dialogue. Writing like Normans or Elizabethans leaves a book unreadable, and pastiche works better for nearer periods. [S2]
- HIS-PIT-06: Do not make past people simple. People in the past were as complex as people today, and the simple milkmaid is a myth. [S4]

## Reader psychology
Builds on the reader-engagement skill.
- HIS-ENG-01: Every period detail in a scene is tied to a character action, reaction or choice in that scene. [S3]
  Check: for each period detail, the same scene shows a character using it, reacting to it or deciding because of it.
- HIS-ENG-02: No stretch of historical explanation runs past 100 words without a character beat or line of dialogue (house setting). [S3]
  Check: count words in each explanatory passage between character beats; none exceeds 100.
- HIS-ENG-03: Where a true event may seem unbelievable to a modern reader, a character voices doubt and another confirms it is true. [S3]
  Check: for each unbelievable true event, one line shows a character's disbelief and another character confirming it.
- HIS-ENG-04: Every action or belief of the protagonist that departs from period norms meets an on-page obstacle, cost or reaction. [S3]
  Check: for each departure from norms, the text shows who objects or what it costs in that scene or the next.
- HIS-ENG-05: Each word in dialogue that sounds modern has been checked for first use before the setting year, so a wrong word does not break the reader's immersion. [S2][S4]
  Check: the notes list each flagged word with a first-use date earlier than the setting year, or the word is replaced.
- HIS-ENG-06: When a real person appears, the thoughts and motives given to them fill gaps in the record and do not contradict documented acts (house setting). The source urges staying tethered to the record but leaves the line to the author. [S5]
  Check: for each real person shown, list the documented acts in the scene; none is reversed, moved or denied by their on-page thoughts.

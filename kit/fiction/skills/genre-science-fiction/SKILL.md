# Science fiction

Layers on top of the audience profile. Where this skill and the audience profile disagree on length or content limits, the audience profile wins.

## Reader promise
- SF-PROM-01: Readers come for a world that unfolds in their heads as they read. Having a world unfold is called the fundamental science fiction experience, with the reader helping to build it from clues. [S3]
- SF-PROM-02: Science fiction extrapolates in ways that can be questioned. If none of a story's assumptions could be challenged, it would not be science fiction. [S1]
- SF-PROM-03: A science fiction novel still succeeds for the reasons any good novel does: connection, consistency and characters the reader cares about. [S1]
- SF-PROM-04: Commercial science fiction is bought for a captivating voice, a quick pace and a primarily character-driven narrative. One Orbit editor lists space opera, adventure, near-future, cyberpunk, and stories with AI or aliens as interests. [S7]

## Structure
- SF-STR-01: Release technical information gradually, so that each piece feels inevitable. Done well this builds more tension than giving everything at the start. [S1]
- SF-STR-02: Open on a character in a scene. An agent names a science fiction opening that spends two pages describing the strange landscape as one he dislikes. [S6]
- SF-STR-03: Writer's Digest calls 100,000 to 115,000 words an excellent range for science fiction and fantasy, with 90,000 to 100,000 and 115,000 to 124,000 probably all right. Long debuts are risky, so do not exceed 124,000 words without a recorded reason (house setting). [S5]
- SF-STR-04: Let the idea drive consequences for people. A critique of one author's later novels is that plots built only to explore an idea reach the mind but fail to fire the emotions. [S4]
- SF-STR-05: Introduce culture, technology and politics when plot and character need them, and let the world show through how it has shaped the characters. [S2]

## Conventions
- SF-CONV-01: Anchor each invention to a checkable fact about the real world, like a subtle footnote inside the text. [S1]
- SF-CONV-02: Keep the facts consistent with each other. For a planet with twice Earth's mass, ask about gravity, atmosphere, year length, the build of the animals and what predators eat. Biology is often neglected. [S1]
- SF-CONV-03: Scatter information through the text (incluing) instead of stopping the story to give it. Modern science fiction assumes the world and does not stop to explain, for example, that a red and a blue sun mean a binary system. [S3]
- SF-CONV-04: A naive newcomer or a specialist character can carry explanation, as long as the explanation stays unobtrusive, flows with the story and the plot keeps moving. [S1][S2]
- SF-CONV-05: Define the limits of a power or technology, or a plot hole can open (the source's example is a wizard's foresight). Do not over-explain either, because three pages on why dollars are ducats read like an economics textbook. [S2]
- SF-CONV-06: Use jargon sparingly and for effect, like foreign words, and restate expert terms plainly. Keep a term that takes on meaning in the story. [S1]

## Pitfalls
- SF-PIT-01: Lecturing is the biggest mistake in explaining science. Show the science through the story, and write the first draft long, then cut hard. [S1]
- SF-PIT-02: Do not open with a long description of the landscape or setting. [S6]
- SF-PIT-03: Do not explain both too much and not enough. Writers who do not know what needs explanation end up with old-fashioned, reinvent-the-wheel exposition that annoys science fiction readers. [S3]
- SF-PIT-04: Do not let characters exist only to illustrate the idea. Where ideas support the human story, as in the Vinge example, the balance holds. [S4]
- SF-PIT-05: Do not lean on a stock device. Physicists told one author that an anti-gravity device would be dismissed as a cliche, while a white hole as an energy source might be taken seriously. [S1]
- SF-PIT-06: Do not give a long technical or political monologue. A fifty-two paragraph monologue about Mars, Earth and the Belt is the source's example of an infodump to avoid. [S2]

## Reader psychology
Builds on the reader-engagement skill.
- SF-ENG-01: No technical or world explanation runs past 120 words without a character action, reaction or line of dialogue interrupting it (house setting). [S1][S2]
  Check: count the words in each explanatory passage between character beats; none exceeds 120.
- SF-ENG-02: A new technology or social rule first appears in use, broken or suffered by a character, and only afterward, if at all, is it explained. [S3][S2]
  Check: for each new technology or rule, the first mention is inside an action or reaction, not inside a definition sentence.
- SF-ENG-03: Each major invention changes a named character's choice, relationship or risk in at least one scene. [S1][S4]
  Check: name the scene and the character whose decision, bond or danger the invention alters.
- SF-ENG-04: The first paragraph names or shows a character doing something, and setting description on the first page serves that action. The first page also shows what the character wants or risks (house setting). [S6]
  Check: the first paragraph contains a character and a verb of action or perception; no description block opens the book; the first page shows a want or risk.
- SF-ENG-05: Each technical reveal is followed in the same scene by a plot consequence, a new question or a decision, so information arrives in steps that keep tension. [S1]
  Check: after each technical reveal, the same scene shows a consequence or poses a new question.
- SF-ENG-06: Unfamiliar terms can be understood from the sentences around them, so the reader assembles the world from clues. [S3]
  Check: each invented term's first use has a context clue within the same or the next paragraph (house setting).

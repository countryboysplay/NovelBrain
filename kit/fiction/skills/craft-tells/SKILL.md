# Craft: Prose tells

These are craft principles, not formulas. The book's voice sheet (`bible/world/style.md`) and its audience and genre skills override them: a pattern the voice sheet uses on purpose is voice, not a tell. One instance proves nothing; most of these phrases have been in human-written fiction for decades. What makes prose read as machine-made or tired is density and recurrence, so the checks count.

## Principles
- CT-PRIN-01: A model's first draft drifts toward one narrow, repetitive, instantly recognisable style that resembles no single human author. Treat the first phrasing that arrives as the one to question. [S2] [S3]
- CT-PRIN-02: A single stock phrase is not a fault on its own. A copy chief found "let out a breath she didn't know she was holding" in dozens of human-written books and would flag it only when it recurs in one manuscript. Judge tells by how many there are and where they fall. [S4] [S1]
- CT-PRIN-03: First ideas tend to be everyone's first ideas: predictable, cliché, flat. The fresher choice usually appears only after the obvious ones are listed and set aside. [S6]
- CT-PRIN-04: Telling the reader what dialogue and action have already shown weakens both. Cut the explanation; if the feeling no longer shows, rewrite the moment so that it does. [S5]
- CT-PRIN-05: A comparison should come from the person who makes it. A narrator's choice of image characterises that narrator; a throwaway, sourceless simile, or an abstraction given a texture, taste or weight, reads as decoration. [S2] [S3]
- CT-PRIN-06: A flashy line every few sentences cancels itself out. Spend striking images where the scene needs them and let plain sentences carry the rest. [S3]

## Techniques
- CT-TECH-01: The lesser-writer pass. Before drafting each beat, name to yourself the version a lesser writer would produce: the expected gesture, the stock image, the obvious line of dialogue, the outcome the reader already predicts. Then write something different that is still true to the character, the plan and the voice sheet (house setting: one alternative per beat at least). [S6]
- CT-TECH-02: Replace the generic body signal (breath, jaw, heart, stomach, hands through hair) with an action or perception only this character, in this place, would have. [S4] [S6]
- CT-TECH-03: Run a search pass for the catalogue under Pitfalls and treat each hit as a question, not an automatic cut: keep it only if the voice sheet wants it or nothing else would do. [S7]
- CT-TECH-04: End a scene on an action, an image, a line of dialogue or an open question, and leave before the characters or the narrator explain what it meant (house setting). [S8] [S5]

## Pitfalls
- CT-PIT-01: Stock significance phrasing: "a testament to", "stands as", "serves as", "a pivotal moment", "a crucial role", "an indelible mark", "deeply rooted", "the weight of", and the vocabulary "tapestry", "intricate", "vibrant", "profound", "delve", "underscore", "showcase", "enduring", "nestled", "in the heart of". [S1]
- CT-PIT-02: Negative parallelism used as a reveal: "It wasn't X. It was Y.", "not X but Y", "not just X, but Y", "no X, no Y, just Z", and the reversed "Y rather than X". [S1] [S3]
- CT-PIT-03: Reflexive triads: three adjectives, clauses or images by habit where one would do. [S1]
- CT-PIT-04: A present-participle clause tacked onto a sentence to comment on its meaning ("..., a reminder that nothing would be the same"). [S1]
- CT-PIT-05: The image rolodex: shadows, echoes, whispers, voids, heartbeats and pulses used as all-purpose images in any scene. [S3]
- CT-PIT-06: Similes that do not hold and abstract-concrete pairings: an abstraction given a texture, taste or weight ("sorrow tasted of metal"), a comparison with no source in the viewpoint character, an image dropped as soon as it is made. [S2] [S3]
- CT-PIT-07: Stock body language: a breath held without knowing, hands dragged through hair, a clenched jaw, an arched eyebrow, a hammering heart, a dropping stomach. Tired rather than wrong; a fault when they recur. [S4] [S7]
- CT-PIT-08: Explain-after-show: a gesture or line shows the feeling and the next sentence names it ("She slammed the drawer. She was furious."), or a dialogue-tag adverb labels what the line already carries. [S5]
- CT-PIT-09: Hedged interiority: "something she couldn't name", "a feeling she didn't understand", "something shifted", where the scene should commit to what the character feels or notices (house setting: RUE applied to vagueness). [S5]
- CT-PIT-10: Characters who explain their own psychology fluently ("I push people away because I'm afraid they'll leave") instead of acting it out (house setting). [S5]
- CT-PIT-11: Everyone equally articulate: every speaker as fluent, wry and quotable as the narrator, so the voices merge into one house style (house setting). [S2]
- CT-PIT-12: The summarising close: a scene's last paragraph states what the scene meant, what the character learned or how things now stand (house setting). [S8]
- CT-PIT-13: Eyeball kicks and typographic emphasis: a striking image in nearly every sentence, or the last word of a sentence italicised for effect. [S3]

## Checks
- CT-CHK-01: Tell density is low (house settings: the thresholds). [S1] [S4]
  Check: count every instance of CT-PIT-01 to CT-PIT-13 in the scene, quoting each, and leave out what the voice sheet uses on purpose; fail if there are 4 or more per 1,000 words of the scene, or 2 or more in its first 250 words or in its last paragraph.
- CT-CHK-02: No feeling is named after the scene has shown it. [S5]
  Check: for each sentence of narration or each dialogue tag that names an emotion, read the two sentences before it; fail if they already show that emotion through action, gesture or dialogue.
- CT-CHK-03: The scene does at least one thing a reader would not predict (house setting). [S6]
  Check: state in one sentence the most predictable way this scene's central beat could go, then quote where the scene does something else that is still true to the character; fail if every beat goes exactly the predictable way.
- CT-CHK-04: Comparisons belong to the viewer. [S2] [S3]
  Check: list each simile and metaphor and name what in the viewpoint character's life, work or place it comes from; fail any with no such source, and any abstract-concrete pairing, unless the voice sheet allows it.
- CT-CHK-05: No tic repeats (house setting: the count). [S7] [S4]
  Check: list every gesture, body reaction, image and distinctive phrase used twice or more, in the scene and, when you read them, in the chapter's earlier scenes; fail if any appears three or more times.
- CT-CHK-06: The scene does not end on a summary (house setting). [S8]
  Check: quote the last paragraph; fail if it states what the scene meant, what the character learned or how things now stand instead of ending on an action, image, line or open question, unless the voice sheet or the plan calls for a reflective close.
- CT-CHK-07: Characters act out their psychology and do not all sound alike (house setting). [S5] [S2]
  Check: list lines in which a character explains their own motives in abstract terms, and name how two speakers' lines differ in vocabulary or rhythm; fail on a self-diagnosis with no dramatic reason, or if no difference can be named.
- CT-CHK-08: Sentence openings vary (house setting: passages and threshold; S7 recommends targeted search passes for repeated patterns). [S7]
  Check: in three passages of about 300 words, from the start, middle and end of the scene or chapter, count the sentences that open with the same word (a pronoun, The, As, a name); fail if one opening word starts more than 40 percent of the sentences in two of the three passages, unless the voice sheet calls for it.
- CT-CHK-09: Consecutive scenes do not share one shape (house setting; S2 describes model fiction's narrow, repetitive shape). [S2]
  Check: give each scene of the chapter its shape in a few words (for example arrive, talk, realise, reflect) and its kind of ending; fail if three scenes in a row share both.

# New adult (about ages 18-25)

New adult is mostly a marketing and shelving label, not a reading level. Traditional publishing often files these books as adult or upper YA, and in practice the label has narrowed to romance and romantasy about people in their late teens and twenties. Treat it as a theme-and-heat profile on top of an adult-level prose profile.

## Length
No reliable source gives a new adult chapter length or a word-count standard for the label itself; only the genre bands below and one writers' organisation's "up to 120,000" ceiling are sourced.

- NA-LEN-01: Treat new adult as a marketing label, not a reading level or a fixed length class. Set length from the genre (romance, romantasy, fantasy), not from the label. [S1][S5][S6]
- NA-LEN-02: Contemporary romance runs 70,000-90,000 words total. Stay inside this band unless `bible/world/brief.md` gives a reason, such as a dual timeline. [S2]
- NA-LEN-03: Romantasy and historical romantasy run 90,000-110,000 words total. [S2]
- NA-LEN-04: Do not use the new adult label to justify extra length. One source (S1) says new adult titles can run up to 120,000 words, and S2 says agents' interest may wane as word count rises (its example is a 110,000-word literary debut) and that overly long romance can drag, so any book above its genre band needs a stated reason in the brief. [S1][S2]
- NA-LEN-05: If the plan splits a long story into several books, each book must close its own central relationship or plot question and also leave room for the series. [S2][S3]

## Language
No reliable source gives sentence length, paragraph length or reading-level numbers for new adult, so the rules here are qualitative.

- NA-LANG-01: Write prose at an adult commercial level. Do not simplify vocabulary or sentence structure the way middle-grade or early YA does; the label signals older readers, not an easier read. [S1][S6]
- NA-LANG-02: The voice must sound like a person of the protagonist's age and stage (for example a first-year student or a first-job twenty-something), with that person's idiom and references. [S9][S1]
- NA-LANG-03: Interior thought carries the emotional weight. The narration looks inward at identity, desire and doubt more than it describes outward events. [S1][S9]
- NA-LANG-04: Swearing, drug and alcohol references and sexual vocabulary may be more explicit than in YA, but they must match the chosen heat tier and stay consistent across the book. [S1][S10]

## POV, tense and protagonist
- NA-POV-01: The protagonist is 18 or older on page one. The core band is 18-25, some sources stretch it to 29 or 30, and none go below 18 for a book labelled new adult. [S5][S6][S1]
- NA-POV-02: The protagonist stands at a threshold of firsts: leaving home, starting college or a first job, living independently, a first serious relationship. State the threshold in the first chapter. [S9][S1][S8]
- NA-POV-03: First person is described as usual for YA and NA by one commentator (S9, single source) and is not an established norm, so choose first or close third from the brief. Pick one POV mode per character and keep it fixed. [S9]
- NA-POV-04: A protagonist aged 18-25 does not by itself make a book new adult. The book also needs the stage-of-life focus (romance, career, worldview, identity) rather than a general study of human behaviour. [S9][S1]

## Content limits
- NA-CON-01: Decide the heat tier before drafting and write it into the Shelf section of `bible/world/brief.md` using named tiers: no kisses, sweet or clean, closed door or fade to black, open door or steamy, high heat or explicit. Nielsen found younger romance readers lean toward erotic romance, so the tier is a real choice. [S10][S1][S4]
- NA-CON-02: Tier labels are not standard across publishers and retailers. Define the book's tier in one sentence (for example "open door, three scenes, moderate detail") instead of relying on the tier word alone. [S10]
- NA-CON-03: Heat level and content warnings are separate. A low-heat book can still hold violence, abuse or grief, so list those in a content note. [S10]
- NA-CON-04: Violence, drugs, strong language and sex are permitted at adult levels, but every character in a sexual scene is 18 or older. This extends the 18+ protagonist norm in S5 and S6 and is kept as a safety floor (house setting). [S5][S6]
- NA-CON-05: Early new adult earned a reputation as thinly disguised erotica. Do not use sexual content as a substitute for plot or character change; every intimate scene must move the relationship or the protagonist's arc. [S1][S6]

## Pacing and themes
- NA-PACE-01: Core themes come from this life stage: independence, college and early career, identity, sexuality, friendships, family strain and romance. Build the main plot on at least one of them. [S1][S6][S8]
- NA-PACE-02: For romance and romantasy, the central love story is the main plot and the ending is emotionally satisfying and optimistic. Readers expect a happily-ever-after or happy-for-now, so do not end on a break-up or the death of a lead. [S11]
- NA-PACE-03: Romantasy splits page time between the romance and the fantasy plot. A low-heat romantasy still needs strong romantic tension in every act. [S10][S3]
- NA-PACE-04: Themes should be more mature and complex than YA's. Include consequences adults face (rent, grades, jobs, debt, estrangement), not only emotional ones. [S1][S8]
- NA-PACE-05: Use tropes named in the brief (for example enemies to lovers, friends to lovers, forbidden love, forced proximity, slow burn, fated mates) and deliver each trope's expected beats. [S3][S7]

## Reader psychology
Builds on the reader-engagement skill.
- NA-ENG-01: The two romantic leads share a scene within the first 3,000 words, and the reason they cannot simply be together is stated by the end of chapter 3 (house setting; direction from the love-story-first definition). [S11]
  Check: chapters 1-3 contain a scene with both leads named, and a sentence or beat that names the obstacle (rule, secret, rivalry, distance or danger). (house setting)
- NA-ENG-02: Name the book's main trope through a concrete event within the first 5,000 words (house setting), not through a label. [S3][S7]
  Check: within the first 5,000 words an event occurs that matches the brief's trope (for forced proximity, the leads are made to share a space). (house setting)
- NA-ENG-03: Every chapter shifts the relationship by at least one step: closer, further, or changed in kind. [S11][S3]
  Check: each chapter contains a line or action after which the pair stands in a different position than at its start (a confession, a touch, a lie, a refusal or a promise).
- NA-ENG-04: Emotional beats alternate with plot beats. No run of more than three scenes is purely plot or purely romantic (house setting; S3 says readers prize emotional payoff). [S3]
  Check: tag each scene "romance beat" or "plot beat" and confirm no run of four or more scenes shares one tag. (house setting)
- NA-ENG-05: The final chapter resolves the central relationship question, and in a series it also leaves one open hook that points to the next book (house setting; S3 shows readers follow series and recurring characters but does not state a hook rule). [S3][S2]
  Check: the last chapter states the lead pair's status (together, committed or happy for now), and if the series flag is set it contains one unanswered question or new threat about the next book.
- NA-ENG-06: Each firsts moment named in the brief (first kiss, first night, first apartment, first job day) is shown as a scene of at least 150 words, not summarised (house setting; S9 and S1 name firsts as the core of the stage). [S9][S1]
  Check: every firsts moment in the brief appears as a scene of at least 150 words containing one physical detail and one stated feeling. (house setting)
- NA-ENG-07: The heat tier, trope list and content notes appear in the front matter or blurb (planned in the Blurb and Shelf sections of `bible/world/brief.md`) so readers know what to expect before they start. [S10][S3]
  Check: the Blurb or Shelf section of `bible/world/brief.md` lists the heat tier, at least one trope, and any content warnings.
- NA-ENG-08: In a series, each book's opening reintroduces the lead and the relationship state in its first 500 words (house setting). S3 shows readers binge series but states no recap rule; the 500-word limit is set to protect readers entering mid-series. [S3]
  Check: the first 500 words name the lead, the relationship state and the stake without needing knowledge of earlier books. (house setting)

## Put-down risks
- NA-RISK-01: Writing too young for adult readers and too graphic for YA readers is a named mistake. Check that voice, themes and content all point to the 18-25 stage and none is mismatched. [S1]
- NA-RISK-02: A heat level that differs from the brief's stated tier, more or less explicit than promised, breaks reader trust. [S10]
- NA-RISK-03: A romance that ends without an optimistic resolution, or sidelines the love story behind subplots, loses the genre's core readers. [S11]
- NA-RISK-04: Over-length hurts. S2 says agents' interest may wane as word counts rise and that overly long romance can drag, so cut scenes that move neither the relationship nor the plot. [S2]
- NA-RISK-05: Trope-loyal readers search by trope. A book that promises a trope and drops it midway risks abandonment. [S3][S7]

## Re-engagement
- NA-RE-01: Open a chapter that follows a long absence or time skip with a one-line reminder of the relationship state and the last unresolved tension (house setting; S3 notes readers follow recurring characters across books). [S3]
- NA-RE-02: After a break-up or major conflict, return the leads to a shared scene or exchange within two chapters (house setting) so the reader's hope for them is renewed. [S11][S3]
- NA-RE-03: In a series, repeat the trope signal from book one in each later book's blurb so returning readers recognise the promise; S3 says discovery depends on blurbs and metadata that spotlight tropes. [S3][S7]
- NA-RE-04: A chapter ending after a slow stretch carries a new question, a reversal or a stated decision that points to the next chapter (house setting; S3 says readers want emotional payoff). [S3]
